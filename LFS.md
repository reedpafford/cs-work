# RUNBOOK — Long-form statics (LFS)
> Trigger: "LFS" · "long-form static" · "write the unaware static" · "transpose this Rosabella LFS." Open this WITH CLAUDE.md and write.md, never instead of them. Everything there still governs. This file holds only what changes when the ad is one photo and a wall of text, written as meta-skills, not as a template. The seven Rosabella pieces studied (UPS · Ghost · Diner · Amish · Truck Stop · Endocrinologist's widow · Cardiac nurse) are evidence of how the skills get used, not the skills themselves. Nothing here is gospel; the market is.
> Reed's principles, 2026-09-16, are §1. §6 is the back end as Reed built it on JC28. Last updated 2026-09-16 (JC28 finished).

## 1. What an LFS is on this account (Reed, 2026-09-16)
- **One image, one very long post.** The image and the first sentence are the hook, the two most important parts by far. The image is candid to the point of looking like a bad phone photo, no context, and that missing context is the mystery. It needs a vibe and a question. ChatGPT image model; Reed's examples to follow. Never a product shot, a stock face, or text on the image.
- **Cost is near zero, so the copy is the whole ad.** Nothing can wreck it (no bad edit, no bad visual) and nothing can carry it either. There is no second element. You cannot half-write it.
- **Testing:** six to eight per ad set (videos get three; static placements are cheaper).
- **Two forms, both filed UA.** *Unaware-indirect:* the first two or three pages have no visible link to the product or the problem, and the product is pivoted in at the back. *Unaware-direct:* the story is about the danger itself; technically PA, but the read is so long and so much story sits in front of the ask that it behaves as UA.
- **Length:** as long as the story needs; some are a twenty-minute read. A boring paragraph is the problem, never the length.
- **Why:** a small slice of the market buys off a direct hook; the wider pool needs a story to bring them in. The story earns the CTA. By the time the product arrives they're warm because they were given something first. The TAM is every woman, so a wide net still lands on a buyer.
- **Taste:** Hallmark (cheesy, predictable, sometimes unrealistic, and older readers love it) plus true crime (women are morbidly curious). Both in one piece: a warm story with a crime inside it. Pull from current events, movies, books, podcasts, real kidnapping and predator cases, reworked.

## 2. First principles for writing one
0. **The goal comes first, and nothing below is gospel (Reed, 2026-09-16).** Don't take the winners, or this file, at face value. Every ad starts from what it has to accomplish: the belief shift, then the engine that carries it, and only then the words, which come naturally once those two are fixed. Some of what looks like a first principle in the seven is just what that engine needed. Every beat has a purpose or it goes. An LFS is still in its most atomic form; it isn't long to be long, it's long because it's packed with copy that does work, and it leaves the reader nothing left to do but buy. **The belief dictates the geometry of the story (JC28).** "Never let them take her to the second location" forced the danger to be the first altercation (a gas station bathroom, four minutes, forty feet from the van) and forced the product to end it right there; the swipe's handoff-at-a-truck-stop could not carry that belief and was rebuilt. Before the first draft, write the belief in one sentence and ask what the story is physically required to show for that sentence to be true..
1. **Words are all you have, so belief has to be built in words.** A face on camera lends credibility for free. Text lends nothing. Every claim needs a reason to be believed that lives on the page: a specific, a concession, a witness, a test the reader can do at the kitchen table. The room is the advantage: an LFS can handle every objection the buyer has, one at a time, at full depth, which a two-minute video never can. That's why they convert. Use the room for that, not for more adjectives.
2. **Somebody in the piece has earned the right to teach.** Authority is anyone the reader will trust and learn from: a detective, a nurse, a mother whose daughter was taken, a paramedic, a farmer who has watched something for sixty years. The narrator can be that person, or the narrator can be ordinary and the authority walks in at the table, or the authority can be the one who failed (the endocrinologist who followed his own protocol and died). Any chair works. What never works is a mechanism with no one behind it. And what never earns its place is an authority restating what the story already showed (JC28, 2026-09-16: a 900-word detective scene cut because Brianna and the front had proved every line of it; one deputy sentence kept, secondhand, so it reads as pattern and not luck). Choose the chair by the buyer: the narrator sits where the buyer sits, the teacher sits wherever the teaching is most believable, and sometimes that's the same person.
3. **One goal, one engine at the macro; a belief chain and an engine chain underneath.** Every ad is still a chain of belief shifts (audit.md step 2), and each shift has its own small engine. But zoomed out, an LFS has one goal (for us: she could be assaulted at any moment, and the alarm is how you stop it) and one story engine carrying it. It isn't complicated. §3 is the engine catalog so far. Pick the macro engine first; let the chain live inside it.
4. **The hook is usually a scene with something slightly off in it, and a promise, but not always.** Three shapes so far. The odd scene with an object (fifty packages rotting on a porch; a daughter in uniform writing her father a ticket). The paradox claim (a surgeon who won't take his own statin; the expert taken apart by the disease he treated). And the plain casual one: "I found a dog in the Walmart parking lot last Tuesday. Small thing. Brown and white." No contradiction at all; the pull is how ordinary it is, and the promise arrives a page later ("if I'd known what was waiting on the other side of that door…"). The hook shape serves the engine, so choose it after the engine, not before. **Both shapes are live (Reed, 2026-09-16).** The casual one was one of Rosabella's biggest spenders; it is not a lesser hook, it is a different engine, and it gets used on purpose when the front is indirect and the pull is that she can't tell yet why she's being told. The knife (keyword first, the impossible sentence after) is what a direct front needs. My JC28 misses weren't casual on purpose, they were scenes with no reason to keep reading. `hooks.md` governs either way: never the product or its price.
5. **Runway scales with how little the reader is looking for the product.** Indirect pieces run 60 to 75 percent of the words before the product is named; the direct ones still spend the first quarter proving the narrator's right to speak and don't name the product until past half. The reader is never addressed as "you" until the last eighth. That's what makes it a story and not an ad.
6. **Objections are answered in character, shown never told.** The narrator voices the reader's skepticism ("It was a beet. I almost laughed."), the authority answers it, and the reader never has to raise it herself. List the objections before drafting. How each one gets conveyed depends on the objection and the engine (a scene, a line of dialogue, a receipt, a concession), but it is always shown, never stated as a claim. See §4 for the bank.
7. **Motive is stated once, plainly.** Every winner has a paragraph that says why this person is writing: "I'm not a salesperson. I don't get paid. That's the whole reason. There isn't another one." The reader needs to know what the writer gets out of it, and the answer has to be nothing but the story.
8. **The prose reads like a real person sat down and wrote it well.** Not fragmented speech, not a voiceover, not filler. No "um," no stumbles; those were for the ear. Natural written English, short paragraphs, real dialogue, digits, a jagged edge. It can be better written than the person would talk, because writing is. Comprehensible on one read at the sixth-grade level still holds, and so does the roller coaster (CLAUDE.md principle 7): the page needs the same unpredictable altitude, length, and emotion as the spoken script, just without the spoken tics.
9. **The pitch is short and the P.S. stack sells.** The body ends on a scene and a few plain lines. The offer, the guarantee, the scarcity, the bundle, the caregiver instruction, and the last knife all live in five to seven postscripts, each with one job. Every LFS gets one.
10. **Build one deep back end, then rinse it.** Rosabella wrote its mechanism, its failed-cheap-product beat, its four criteria, its felt-proof-versus-real-proof line, and its P.S. stack once and reused them under seven different fronts. Our version of that module doesn't exist yet. It comes to fruition as Reed writes the first ones by hand; every edit he makes is logged in `edits.md` and this file gets smarter from it. Fronts are cheap and infinite; the back end is the asset.
11. **Honesty is the key, and anonymization when the story calls for it.** Honest limits ("I couldn't see my scan change in eleven weeks and I won't pretend mine did") buy the claim next to them. "I've changed the names" and "Halloran isn't his name" make the rest read as true, but only when the nature of the story makes hiding something plausible. Never as a tic.

## 3. Engines (story dynamics that carry the belief)
The dynamics behind the words, not the words. Observed so far; the list grows.
- **Two timelines.** A finished tragedy laid next to an open one. The stranger's daughter is gone; mine is still in the house. Imminence without a clock. (UPS, Diner, Truck Stop, the Ghost's Duke.)
- **The expert who couldn't save himself.** The person who knew the most followed every rule and lost anyway, so the rules are the problem, not compliance. Kills "she'll be fine, we did everything right." (Endocrinologist's widow.)
- **The insider who can't tell you.** Someone with knowledge and a reason they can't say it in public tells the narrator off the record, and the narrator passes it on. Pre-answers "why hasn't anyone told me this." (Cardiac nurse.) For us: the officer who can't put it in the report, the campus cop who'd be fired for saying it.
- **The stranger who saved us.** A kindness to a stranger comes back as the thing that saves the narrator's family. Trojan horse: it reads as a story about decency. (Grocery, Diner, the adopted son.)
- **The return to the source.** The narrator goes back to the place or person she ran from and finds the answer was there the whole time. (Amish.)
- **The reunion with stakes.** Someone finally has a reason to live, and the danger arrives at that exact moment. (Ghost, Repo Man.) For us: the daughter who moves home, the granddaughter who arrives, then the night.
- **Proof from the grave.** Records, letters, a chart of someone who died show the pattern before it repeats in the living. (Adopted son's father; the Ghost's Duke.) For us: the missing-poster file, the police report a mother kept.
- **The rescue that reveals.** A dramatic event (the rescue at the gas station) is the front; what it reveals is the back. (Truck Stop.)
Any of these can be indirect (the front is about something else) or direct (the front is the danger). Pick the engine first, then the narrator, then the chair of the authority.

## 4. The believability toolkit (what the room is for)
The objections a buyer has, and the device the winners used to answer each. Plan these before writing; each is a scene.
| Objection in her head | Device | Ours |
|---|---|---|
| "Why hasn't anyone told me this?" | the insider's cage: license, standard of care, "I'd be finished" | the officer off the record; the report that can't say it |
| "We did everything right" | the expert who failed while compliant | the family that had pepper spray, the escort, the phone, and still |
| "It's just aging / it's just college" | the villain named as the mechanism, not a side effect | the freeze as instinct; "we trained the scream out of her" |
| "I tried something and it didn't work" | the cheap version fails first, with a receipt | the Amazon alarm that died in her bag / went off in class |
| "Why this one?" | the criteria grid (strain, drying, dose, lab sheet) | anti-misfire, the drop test, the flashlight, small batch, the detective's pick |
| "Will I feel anything?" | felt proof vs real proof, separated in words | the kitchen test the day it arrives vs the night it's pulled |
| "Is there evidence?" | one trial, one number, after the object has done the work | one number, once, after the story has done the work |
| "Are you selling me?" | the motive paragraph, the "I don't get paid" | not affiliated, just a mother / just a happy dad |
| "What if it doesn't work?" | guarantee vs "nobody refunded Ed" | thirty-day guarantee vs what the school gave her |
| "Is this real?" | changed names ("Halloran isn't his name"), exact times, dollar amounts, named minor characters, honest limits ("I couldn't see my scan change in eleven weeks") | same; hedge small facts, keep the big ones hard |
| "Can I do this to a stubborn person?" | "don't ask him, tell him: just take it, for me" | "put it on her keys yourself" |
| "Why now?" | the disease runs today; scarcity as arithmetic, not a countdown | the red zone; the next weekend; small batches |
**Whose mouth (JC28):** every objection is answered by the person who paid for the answer. The survivor carries the spray-to-alarm switch (the school took hers); the mother who shopped for it carries why-this-one and the pin; the daughter raises the misfire objection as a joke and it's answered in one line; the neighbor who heard the test asks for the name (word of mouth); the roommate asks where she got it (the multi-buy). An authority is one sentence, secondhand, so the story reads as pattern and not luck. Nobody restates what a scene already showed.
Other devices with no objection attached, all of which the room allows: the mechanism taught twice by two different people with two different objects; the day-by-day accumulation of small domestic proofs; a skeptical professional confirming at the end; "read this part slowly"; the anonymization statement; the refrain said three times; the object from the opening returning at the end; the "hinges" paragraph (every moment it could have gone the other way).

## 5. Structure as ratios, not a template
The Rosabella back half ran the same order in every piece because they built it once. Ours will too, but it will be ours. What transfers is the shape, not the beats:
- **Open (0 to 5%):** the contradiction, the promise, the narrator's name and right to speak (one paragraph of who I am, ordinary or expert).
- **Runway (5 to 50%):** the engine's front. Story only. Small gestures, named people, the tragedy planted as a detail and left alone. No "you." One withheld line, announced.
- **Turn (around half):** the recognition. One sentence, then silence in the room.
- **Teaching (50 to 65%):** the authority, an object, the narrator arguing back, the mechanism landing.
- **Believability (65 to 80%):** objections as scenes, the failed cheap thing, the criteria, the name, the at-home test, felt vs real proof.
- **Proof (80 to 90%):** accumulation over days and weeks, the full outcome story, the outsider confirms.
- **Close (90 to 95%):** the callback object, the hinges, the first "you," the segment ladder, a pitch under two hundred words, link, signature.
- **P.S. stack (95 to 100%):** the sale.
Percentages are the observed range, not a law. A direct piece compresses the runway and lengthens the teaching. An indirect piece does the reverse.
**JC28 as measured (direct, 2,680 words, 12-minute read):** the object is first seen in a girl's fist at 26% (one sense, one line) · the survivor tells it at the table from 47% · the word "alarm" at 60% (in her mouth, folded into the spray line) · "Palace Guard" at 62%, said by the mother who bought it · the recognition at 68% (felt, then voiced by the other mother, then turned inward on Kayla: three lines, three chairs) · the first "you" at 85% · pitch, motive, link, signature, five P.S. from 90%. Rosabella's winners are three to four times longer; whether ours should be is an open item (§10) until the account reads JC28.

## 6. Our back end (built once on JC28, 2026-09-16; rinse it, then let the account edit it)
This is the module as Reed left it after eight passes. Rinse the order; rewrite the words to the new front.
0. **The authority's explanation earns the product (Reed, JC29, 2026-09-16).** Sequence: what the mother did right → the one authority explanation of why it still failed (no fight, a hand over her mouth, the whole thing turns on whether anybody hears it) → her conclusion ("I bought her the wrong thing") → "so I went and found the loud thing" → the keys off the hook → the object → the name. The mechanism comes before the object so the object is the answer to a question the reader already has. On JC28 the object came first because the survivor was at the table and had pulled it herself; when the authority is the mother who lost, the explanation goes first.
1. **The object comes out of her pocket before anyone explains it (when the survivor is in the room).** The survivor tells the danger scene at the table in her own words (the polite man at the end of the hallway, the hand over her mouth, the freeze: "I just— I just couldn't"), then "But my keys were still in my pocket," then she puts the little white thing on the table. The reader sees it in a girl's hand before a word of teaching.
2. **The switch, in the survivor's mouth.** Pepper spray praised, taken by the school a month before, "so Mom put the alarm on there instead." One line does both jobs. The van and the distance ("forty feet") land here.
3. **The mother who bought it names it and says why this one.** She looked for two weeks; most go off in a purse and end up in a drawer or die on the first drop; the pin doesn't stop until it goes back in; it won't go off in her bag. The product is logo-less, so the name is only ever spoken by someone who shopped for it.
4. **One authority sentence, secondhand.** "The ones that go missing forever are the ones nobody heard." Pattern, not luck. Never a scene.
5. **The recognition, three lines, three chairs.** Felt in the narrator's body ("I put my coffee down because my hand wasn't steady"), voiced by the other mother ("I never actually thought she'd need to use it"), turned inward ("Then I thought about my Kayla"), and then the narrator's daughter's real routine, with the station named. One turn, at the table, in the moment. Never a second one after the guests leave.
6. **The copy cue.** The mother clips it back on her daughter's keys and the narrator watches "the way you watch somebody do something you're about to copy." Ordered that night.
7. **It arrives.** Size and weight against an object she knows (her car key, my thumb), clipped on by the parent, hung on the hook.
8. **The test at home, with a witness.** The husband pulls the pin in the garage; the sound described by failing to describe it; the neighbor is already in his driveway and asks for the name (he has granddaughters).
9. **The daughter's objection as a joke, answered in one line.** "What if it goes off in my lecture hall?" → the real tug, the pin goes back in, two seconds. Anti-misfire is always carried here, never in a P.S.
10. **Proof with a threat in the frame.** Weeks later, two men by the dumpster look at her, and her hand goes to her keys "like checking for a seatbelt." Nobody told her to. The roommate asks; two more ordered.
11. **The segment ladder with the counter-objections inside it.** "You might be where I was three weeks ago" · she's smart and does boxing on Wednesdays (kills "she'd fight back") · the mother can't be in the bathroom for her · the grandmother knows that better than anyone · the father who bought spray: good, keep it, the school took Brianna's, and you can't take it on planes.
12. **The belief line, then our line.** "The only reason Denise got to see her daughter again was because of the one thing she put on her keys." / "An ambulance siren in her pocket."
13. **Motive, link, signature.** Not affiliated; who I am in one sentence; "I couldn't keep this to myself and look another mother in the eye." Found the link. Name.
14. **Five P.S., one new job each.** The offer as a receipt ("bought three, got two free, less than twenty dollars each, I've paid more for a car wash," and who each one went to) · the guarantee against what the school never offered · the text callback ("leaving now" at 10:52, then and now) · scarcity as arithmetic plus the segment clock (drives, closes, walks after dark, leaves for school this month) · the last knife: the line the narrator couldn't put in the story, the survivor's one line to the rescuer, the regret beat, "Don't wait until it's too late." Then 👉 the link.
What Reed cut and never wants back: the hinges paragraph, a professional confirming at the end, a second turn, a recap P.S., the narrator restating what a scene showed, the deliberation beat between the threat and the sound, any pre-announcement of the back half.
Rules that carry over unchanged: mechanisms match the hardware; the invented room is the psychology and the category; the accusation stays as charged on real cases; the dream outcome is never two lines; the regret beat is the last thing before the link.

**Transposing a winning video into an LFS (JC42 from JC31, JC43 from JC30, Reed 2026-09-30: "your additions were really good, keep it up").** Keep every beat of the video in order, then deepen with what a video can't hold: (1) the authority's explanation as three "why do you think" questions, each tied to a detail the reader already saw in the scene (why he went to the girls, why he opened with a joke, why he kept looking over his shoulder), and one of them kills a belief the video never touched (politeness is what he screened for); (2) small true-sounding scene details (the egg on the asphalt, the keys in her coat pocket the whole time, the slippers); (3) an aftermath in three images, one per family member (the baby's diaper bag, the little one takes her hand without asking, the teen's hand goes to her strap with a man too close); (4) the segment ladder built from this story's own beliefs; (5) five P.S. with one job each, the last one carrying the regret beat, the share CTA and "Don't wait." The offer lives only in the first P.S. The hooks come from the video's own proven hooks.

## 7. On the page (where the video rules bend)
- **One visual per line, and each line reads cleanly into the next (Reed, JC28, 2026-09-16).** A line is one picture she can hold; the next line is the next picture. If a line carries two images, split it where the picture changes. If a line carries none, it's explaining, and it goes or gets an object.
- **Spacing is the shape of the ad (Reed, 2026-09-16).** Every paragraph is one complete thought and every paragraph is followed by a blank line; that is how the Rosabella winners sit in the Ad Library (a 9,200-word post is ~380 paragraphs, ~24 words each: a quarter under ten words, a quarter eleven to twenty, a third twenty-one to thirty-five, a sixth longer). Dialogue is always its own paragraph. The right edge reads as a roller coaster: no four paragraphs of the same length in a row outside a dialogue exchange. On the Notion page the blank line is a zero-width-space block (`finalize.md` step 4) so the post pastes into Meta with the spacing intact.
- **The first time the alarm appears, it's only what the witness perceived.** One sense, one line, then the story moves. The depth (the strobe, the pin, the minutes) comes later from the authority. Describing the product in full on its first appearance is the ad showing through (Reed, JC28).
- **The product is logo-less (Reed, JC28).** The name is never read off the device. A character who shopped for it says it, once, with the reason she knows it.
- Paragraphs of one to three sentences; on a phone each is a breath with air under it.
- Dialogue carries the middle, short lines, little attribution, one beat of action between exchanges.
- Digits are receipts (3:47 AM, $11.40, ten cents short). Spelled-out numbers are for the ear.
- Fragments and em-dashes are allowed as rhythm and breath; the only rule is variety (CLAUDE.md principle 7). Half the sentences in the winners are five words or shorter and it never reads as choppy, because the other half are long.
- No filler, no stumbles. Hedges on small facts still read as memory.
- No headers, bullets, bold, emojis, or "click here" in the body. The only formatting is paragraph breaks, the link, the signature, and the P.S. labels.
- One refrain per piece.

## 8. The P.S. stack (five to seven, one job each)
A postscript earns its place only if it adds something not already on the page: a fact, an offer, or a knife. And it carries one idea, with nothing for the reader to work out (JC29: a two-text, two-time callback was cut as "hard to understand"; the fix was the outcome in one line, "I slept through her shift," then the object). A P.S. that recaps a scene the body already played is bloat, and the reader feels it as the ad starting (JC28, 2026-09-16: six recap P.S. cut to five, each new).
guarantee framed against what the system never offered · felt proof vs real proof, honestly · the safety line (anti-misfire; the pin goes back in) · the caregiver's instruction (for the grandmother; for the father who bought spray) · scarcity as arithmetic + the bundle · the last callback with the knife in it (the regret beat) · the link again.

## 9. Before it goes anywhere
- Engine named. Belief named in one sentence. Narrator's chair and the authority's chair named.
- Objection list written; each has a scene.
- Word count at first problem mention, first product mention, first "you." Compare to §5.
- The motive paragraph exists.
- The daughter's night is in full.
- Pitch under two hundred words. P.S. stack complete, link at the end.
- No formatting in the body. Image brief written (candid, no text, a question in it).
- Meta: the image is what gets reviewed; keep the text honest to the story and the image benign.
- **My LFS tells, from JC28's eight passes. Run this before the draft goes to Reed; every one of these was cut by hand:**
  1. The product described in depth the first time it appears (strobe, pin, minutes). One sense, one line.
  2. Two visuals in one line. Split where the picture changes.
  3. A deliberation beat between the threat and the sound. Nothing sits there.
  4. Pre-announcing the back half ("and then let me tell you what she told me a week later"). The hook pulls; the promise device is dead weight.
  5. An authority restating what the story showed. One sentence, secondhand, or nothing.
  6. A second turn after the guests leave. One turn, at the table.
  7. The narrator restating a scene in summary ("It got a sixteen-year-old out of a hallway…"). Cut.
  8. A P.S. that recaps. Every P.S. carries a new fact, offer, or knife.
  9. Rosabella devices imported because they were there (hinges, the professional confirming, the criteria grid). Import a device only when an objection in §4 needs it.
  10. A hook that is a scene with no reason to keep reading. Casual is fine when it's chosen for an indirect front on purpose (the Walmart dog); a knife is what a direct front needs. Either way, never the product or a price.
  11. Two hops where one will do ("her mother called Dave, and Dave called us").
  12. The objection answered by the wrong mouth (Denise repeating the spray line Brianna already said).
