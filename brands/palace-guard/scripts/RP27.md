## Body
### Script
Owen is six, he has autism, and he doesn't talk. He's never once said my name.
So if he ever got lost, he couldn't tell anybody who he was or where he lives, and if you called for him, he wouldn't turn around.
And I knew it was coming, because he's been trying to get out since the day he could walk.
We changed the locks on the front door, and he figured those out in about a week.
My husband put a latch at the very top of the door, where Owen couldn't reach it. We put up one of those little chimes that dings when the door opens.
We even got him a GPS tracker for his wrist, forty dollars a month, and he chewed through the band the first day.
Some nights I slept on the floor outside his room, but that week I'd finally gone back to my own bed.
I woke up a little after three, and the house was cold.
His bed was empty, and the front door was standing wide open.
He'd pushed a kitchen chair over to the door, climbed up, and undone the latch. I guess the chime went off, and I slept right through it.
I ran out barefoot screaming his name, and he doesn't answer to his name. I knew that. I did it anyway.
There's a pond about three blocks behind our house.
I found him standing at the edge of the water in his pajamas, up to his ankles, looking at the moon on it.
It gets deep about two steps out from where he was standing.
I carried him home and sat on the kitchen floor holding him until the sun came up.
The next day a neighbor told me, nicely, that I needed to keep a better eye on him. I hadn't slept a whole night in four years.
A couple weeks later I saw a video of a young man with autism talking about kids who run, and he said something I can't stop thinking about.
He said we're not running away from you, we're running toward something. The water, a light, a sound we like.
That's when it hit me. Every lock I bought was trying to keep him in, and Owen was always going to find a way around a lock, because he had a reason.
What I needed was to know the second he got out.
So I got a personal alarm, the kind girls put on their keys. You pull the pin, and it's louder than an ambulance siren.
I hung it up high on the outside of his bedroom door frame and tied a little string from the pin to his door, so if that door opens at night, the pin comes out.
And I know what some of you are thinking, a siren, for him? Owen covers his ears when I run the vacuum.
That's exactly why it's up high and outside his room, never on him, and the second I push the pin back in, it's quiet. It takes about three seconds.
Three weeks later, at two in the morning, it went off.
I was out of bed before I was even awake, and Owen was halfway down the hallway with his shoes in his hand.
He never made it to the front door. I pushed the pin back in, and I held him on the hallway floor until he calmed down, and I cried, because I'd actually been asleep.
And when we go out, I keep another one clipped on my purse, with the string on his belt loop.
If he takes off in Walmart, everyone in that store is made aware of what's happening, and they see a mom running after a little boy, and somebody stops him.
It's called Palace Guard. There's no app, no monthly fee, and nothing he has to wear on his wrist. It's even got an anti-misfire feature, so it only goes off when that pin actually comes out.
I bought three and got two free, so it came out to less than twenty dollars each. One's on his bedroom door, one's on the back slider, one's on my purse, and the other two went to my mother's house, because that's where we spend the holidays.
New house, a door nobody's watching, everybody thinking somebody else has him. That's when these kids get out.
Most of the kids they don't find in time, they find near water. I got lucky once, and I wasn't going to count on being lucky twice.
