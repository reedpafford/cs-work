## Memo
Why: no brief has spoken to the mother of a non-verbal child, whose fear runs every day, not just in the red zone: her child can't tell her what happened, so she lives on bruises, a school that says he's fine, and her gut. A new cohort with the brand's same belief (nobody hears him, the alarm makes the noise for him). · What: a mother whose non-verbal son keeps coming home with bruises the school waves off tells every mom like her what that's like, and why she clipped an alarm on his backpack so he has a voice when she can't be there. · How: Reed's rough draft (2026-11-24); the short mother talking head, RP20's product block adapted from a daughter to a son.
Avatar: mothers (30s to 50s, and grandmothers) of a non-verbal or autistic child, school age · Sub-avatar: the mom who's already found a bruise nobody could explain and been told by the school that he's in the best care possible.
Desire: to know that when she isn't in the room, somebody will still hear her child.
Angle: these kids get picked because they can't tell; the people who hurt them are counting on his silence, so the one thing they can't plan for is a sound he can make without words.
Mechanism: attention. The alarm is his voice: one pull and every adult in the hallway, the bus or the bus stop knows something is wrong, and the person hurting him can't count on nobody finding out. He uses it like the help button on the iPad he already talks with, so he doesn't have to understand danger, only know he's being hurt.
Authority: the mother herself (she lives it every day), and the Facebook story of a school aide as the proof it happens.
Route: brand formula (the mother, talking head).

### Script
Moms, I'm worried someone is hurting my non-verbal son when I'm not around.
My boy’s name is Joshua, and he’s the sweetest boy you’ll ever meet.
He lines his cars up by color on the windowsill every morning, and he'll hug the cashier at Walmart like he's known her his whole life.
He doesn't believe anybody in this world would ever hurt him.
But for the last couple of months, he's been coming home with bruises. Not on his shins, where a little boy bumps into things, but on his upper arms, like somebody grabbed him.
And he's sad in a way he can't explain to me. He used to hum all day long, you could hear him all the way down the hall. He’s stopped doing that now.
His daily sheet from school says great day, with a smiley face, and I'm sitting there looking at a bruise the size of a grown man's thumb.
I've called the school more times than I can count. They tell me he's in the best care possible, and that he probably did it to himself.
And maybe it isn't happening at school. Maybe it's the kids at the bus stop, where there aren't any teachers around. I don't know, and that's the worst part.
If you're the mom of a kid like mine, you already know what this is like.
It's watching every door, holding his hand a little tighter in the parking lot, and counting heads every minute you're out of the house.
It's knowing he could walk right into traffic, or walk off with a stranger, because he doesn't understand what could happen.
And it's exhausting. Other moms get to loosen their grip as their kids get older, but Joshua's going to get bigger, and his understanding of danger might never catch up.
So no, we're not being paranoid. When your child can't see danger, you have to see it for him, every single day.
But we can't be there every minute, and that's exactly what the people who do this are counting on.
They pick kids like ours because they know our kids can't tell anyone.
A few weeks ago I read a story on Facebook about a school aide who got caught on camera hitting and dragging kids just like Joshua. It went on for months, and not one of those kids could go home and tell their mom.
I didn't sleep that night.
I can't send him to school with a camera in his backpack, they won't allow it. And the AirTag in his shoe tells me where he is, never who he's with.
But I found something that gives him a voice when he'd need it most.
I clipped a personal alarm on Joshua's backpack. I'd seen them all over Facebook, mostly for women, to keep them from being assaulted or trafficked, and I thought, why couldn't it be my baby's voice?
He already uses his iPad to tell me he's hungry, or that he wants Bluey. This is his button for help.
He doesn't have to see it coming. He just has to know it hurts.
Now I know what you're thinking. These things are loud, and for a lot of our kids that's a sensory overload waiting to happen. Joshua wears headphones literally everywhere, so believe me, I thought about it.
I won't act like it's right for every child. But I sat down with Joshua, and we practiced when to pull it and when not to, and it's got an anti-misfire feature, so it won't go off on its own in his backpack.
Hopefully that day never comes.
But if it ever does, if a grown-up grabs him where nobody can see, or a kid pins him down at the bus stop, he pulls one pin, and every adult in that hallway or on that street knows something's wrong.
For the first time, he has a voice.
It's called Palace Guard. It's louder than an ambulance siren, it's got a strobe light, and it's the one thing that can speak for him when I can't be there.
I bought three and got two free, so it came out to less than twenty dollars each.
He’s got one on his backpack, one on his coat, one in his bus bag, one in my purse for the park, and a spare in the kitchen drawer.
They're doing a holiday sale right now, and from what I've seen, they run low this time of year.
I think about the moms in that Facebook story every time I zip up his backpack. They would've given anything for their babies to have a voice.
Share this with every mom of a non-verbal child, so their baby has a voice too.
