# Direct-response ad copywriting playbook

Distilled from the Evolve copywriting course and its Origins program (paid social ads for DTC/e-com brands). Rules first, with short examples. All results quoted are as the instructors reported them, not independently verified.

> **House override (Reed, 2026-11-12):** where this playbook's ethics are stricter than the house line (CLAUDE.md principle 6: the moral line is the value exchange), the house line wins. Dramatized stories, invented personas, fabricated-but-harmless stats, fake scarcity and strong fear are allowed when the buyer has the problem and the product delivers the outcome promised. What's still out: anything that gets someone hurt if believed, a capability the product can't deliver when it matters, a worded hundred-percent guarantee, a checkable false claim about a real person or institution, and anything that breaks the platform's rules (principle 8). Lines below marked *(house)* are edited to match.

---

## 1. Mindset and process

- **Copywriting isn't content writing.** Content informs or entertains. Copy establishes or reinforces a position in the buyer's mind and changes what they do.
- **The market decides.** Never argue about copy; test it. "Really good" and "not converting" don't belong in the same sentence.
- **Research is ~80% of the work.** Two to three hours of research per hour of writing, and research again mid-draft to verify. "The power of your copy is in direct proportion to the depth of your research." With deep research and the customer's own words, even a weak writer can write relatable copy.
- **Don't ship first drafts.** Write, take a break (after lunch, or better, the next day), then rewrite with fresh eyes. Fresh eyes catch "no-shit" headlines, stat mismatches and lines that don't belong. ("Eating 1,200 calories is easy" became "Eating *only* 1,200 calories is easy.")
- **Depth over volume.** About one deep concept a day; a whole day on one video brief is fine.
- **Daily practice (90 days):** a protected hour, phone away. Read one piece of copy and name what it *communicates*, write one ad, log one idea. Keep a "hooks that stopped my scroll" note.
- **Write in the environment.** Draft hooks and statics in a 9:16 canvas (Figma, Canva, Milanote), not a doc. Line breaks and length are copy decisions; a four-line on-screen hook is a failure you only see in context. Templates force shorter copy.
- **Great copy needs boredom.** Original connections come from idle time: walks, phone-free blocks.

## 2. Line-level rules

### The three checks (run on every line)
1. **Can you visualize it?** "Dumpster fire" sticks; "optimize process" doesn't. Let the abstract conclusion form from concrete words: "feels like cashmere, priced like cotton," not "softest shirt." ("1,000 songs in your pocket.")
2. **Can you falsify it?** Could someone call it true or false? "He's smart" can't be; "he scored 1600 on the SAT" can, and it communicates "smart." ("3 egg whites, 6 almonds, 4 cashews, 2 dates.")
3. **Can nobody else say it?** Swap your biggest competitor's product into your ad. If it still makes sense, rewrite. Ownable copy makes them *have* to buy from you.

Three yeses, you're probably scaling. Three nos, it's probably weak.

### Communicate, don't just say
- What copy *communicates* (the feeling) matters more than what it *says*. "They laughed when I sat down at the piano, but when I started to play…" communicates status and the last laugh; "learn to play like a pro" says the same thing and triggers nothing.
- **Iterate a winner on the feeling, not the words.** Name the feeling and desire it creates, then write new words that create the same thing. Most failed "variations" communicate something different, or nothing.

### Facts beat opinions
- Turn opinions into facts. "Unmatched power" → "the first stainless steel humidifier." Facts can't be argued with, communicate more than they say, and build a stream of "that's true" that lets a later opinion land.
- Facts don't have to be statistics: "melts in your mouth, not in your hand."
- **Odd, specific numbers beat round ones:** 12 lbs, 52, 56.4% are more believable than 10 or 50%.

### Delete, speak, simplify
- **Write with the delete key.** A great sentence is a good sentence made shorter. If you can pull a sentence and the flow survives, it didn't belong. Copy is never too long, only too boring; length is fine only when it explains more.
- **Speak it, don't write it.** Every script gets spoken (creator, AI voice, or the reader's inner voice). Read it aloud. Ask: would this help a salesman sell?
- **Grade 6 or lower** (Hemingway app). Line breaks alone can drop the grade from 13 to 5. "Facilitates enhanced metabolic function" → "tells your body to burn fat instead of storing it." "Metabolic gummies" → "gummies for your tummy."

### Customer language and labels
- Use customer quotes, not a thesaurus. "Stressed out" → "completely fried"; "a solution" → "desperate to find something that actually works"; "revolutionary" → "it's a lifesaver."
- Use *their* word: a customer said "smoother and less dry." Nobody said "hydrated," so a "hydrated" angle misses.
- Specific internal monologue beats generic pain: "Has food just become numbers?" beats "Can't control yourself around food." Visual, aggressive verbs: "*Chugging* water instead of eating."
- **Labels:** call people by the names they use for themselves ("light sleeper," "night shifter," "SAHM," "graveyard shift"). A label repels some and strongly pulls the group. You can coin your own and own it.
- Keep terms consistent ("fullness hormone" every time), and pair the emotional term with the clinical one.

### Comparisons
- Metaphors, similes and analogies borrow context and credibility from what the reader already knows, make complex things simple, and reframe why old solutions failed. A metaphor ("a glove for your foot") triggers less skepticism; a simile ("fits like a glove") is clearer but reads more like a claim. ("Using traditional pee pads is like walking the Amazon barefoot.")

### Show, don't tell
- If you can show it, show it ("it's very hard to deny things you can see"), and don't caption what the visual already says.
- If you can't, write experientially. Telling triggers the logical brain:

  | Telling | Showing |
  |---|---|
  | energized | run through a wall |
  | fewer cravings | walk past the vending machine at 3 p.m. without thinking about it |
  | boost your confidence | you'll forget you were ever nervous to go out |
  | high-quality wallet | made with titanium |

- "Half the words, double the examples." The copywriter owns the visual idea, not just the words.

## 3. Before you write

Answer these before the first headline.

### The product
- Every product is two products: the physical one (materials, ingredients) and the functional one (what it does). **People buy what it does.** Don't lead with the tech.
- **Product doc:** how it works (plus a 6th-grade rewrite) → features → benefits → desires (keep asking "why?").
- **Hidden-mechanism questions:**
  - What does it do that you don't advertise?
  - What do you include that competitors don't?
  - "If it worked too well, what would customers complain about?" (A dog harness: "now even I look forward to walks," "everyone asks where I got it.")
  - "How would a customer explain it to a friend?"
- The most common mistake is targeting a desire the product can't live up to.

### Desire
- You can't create desire, only channel it, and only one the product can believably deliver.
- **Sources:** mass instincts (health, status, sex, comfort, control, belonging) and mass tech problems (complexity, overwhelm, fragility, maintenance, incompatibility, obsolescence), shaped by forces of change (trends, new mass education).
- **Headline formula:** instinct or tech problem × trend or new education. Instinct × new education makes the biggest breakthroughs. ("Why are you still choking your dog in 2025?" = health × "collars damage the trachea.")
- **Score desires** on scope (most important), urgency and staying power (does it renew?).
- **Elevation:** move the product up or down the desire ladder to find a less crowded lane, as long as it's authentic. Health brands: everyone sells health, so try control, comfort or sex. (A harness's "stress-free walks" never converted; "take control of every walk" won.)
- **Iterate on the desire, not the words.** "Still choking your dog" (health) and "still wearing collars" (status) look alike and are different ads.
- **Ask "why?" repeatedly** to get under the surface desire (look better → turn heads at the reunion → feel desired again), and show the deep desire throughout the ad.
- **Seasons:** desires peak by season. January = control/health/status. September = routine and fresh start. Buyers shop weeks ahead of holidays.
- **Find desire** in "I want / I need / I was looking for" statements, complaints, trends and where money is spent. Manually first; the golden nuggets are deep in Reddit threads.

### Avatar
- An avatar is "the group most likely to buy," built for relatability ("that's me"). Every ad targets one whether you mean it to or not, and Meta targets through the creative.
- **Build desire-first**, using a surface desire in their words ("I want my dog to stop pulling"), not a core instinct. One desire per avatar.
- **Layer sub-avatars:**
  - **Experience:** situational ("bedroom gets bright in the morning") or product-based ("tried blackout curtains, still woke up tired"). Active experiences create urgency.
  - **Emotion:** secondary emotions (frustrated, overwhelmed) are a signal to dig for the primary one.
  - **Behavior:** the strongest and most underused. Tied to ego and identity; frequency = strength; lets you future-pace. ("If you still meal prep every Sunday…", "chugging water instead of eating.")
  - **Demographic:** last, and only when the product works differently for that group.
- **Beliefs** aren't an avatar category. Unpack them into experience + emotion + behavior. They do make good hooks ("If you think you can't lose weight anymore…").
- A real avatar has emotional pain, past failed attempts, specific goals and daily habits. "Women 35+ with knee pain" is a demographic.
- The broader the avatar, the better the product, offer and copy must be. Without a standout product, go more specific (not alienating).
- Early stage: one core avatar, mined for sub-avatar after sub-avatar. "Out-avatar" competitors on a desire they're already proving.
- **Find sub-avatars** by Googling behavior + "reddit" and reading threads with hundreds of comments. Competitor ads reveal sub-avatars and labels too.

### Angle
- **Angle = the main reason you give this person to buy** = emotional trigger + (daily struggle OR ideal outcome).
- **Every ad needs an angle.** "Us vs them," "3 reasons," "before/after," post-it notes are formats or concepts. Ask "…of what?" If a proven template fails for you, it's what you put in it: the angle.
- **Angles come from sub-avatars, not features.** Product → desire → core avatar → sub-avatars → angle. Extract it as desire + current behavior + gap (a nose-strip sleeper: "better than nose strips," or "this + nose strips = the best sleep of your life").
- **Rule of one:** one avatar, one desire, one message per ad, and the landing page has to match.
- Default to **direct** hooks and headlines that state the angle plainly ("14+ hours, no swelling," "This is a meal, not a protein shake") until you're advanced.

### Mechanism
- **New and different beats better.** The mechanism's job is to inspire hope ("this isn't like everything else") and make you the only place that has it. Familiar messages get filed under "another one of those" and ignored.
- Every claim gets asked: **"what mechanism proves it?"** Visual mechanisms are easiest to believe.
- **Three types, in order:**
  1. **New:** truly proprietary, and it must be *useful*, not just new (a stainless-steel humidifier: easy to clean, boilable, less mold).
  2. **Unspoken:** something everyone does but nobody says ("It's toasted").
  3. **Renamed:** make the ordinary new ("bone broth" vs. stock).
- **"The mechanism does all the work."** Write it so the mechanism is the reason the claim is true: "this [mechanism] is what lets you…". Don't over-explain it technically.
- Keep it simple: "1,000 songs in your pocket." Borrowed credibility works: "nature's Ozempic."
- **Never categorize yourself** ("like X but Y," "Dyson but affordable," "not like other collagens"). Own a new subcategory through the mechanism: "the first supplement that *protects* your fullness hormone, not just boosts it."

### New information
- For commodity products, new information builds authority: the real cause, why past fixes failed. It must be deep, true, fact-checked and relevant to *your* product (you can't attack powders if you sell powder). "Stomach issues? There's a soup for that."
- Education alone doesn't differentiate. Basic information gets "no shit, Sherlock." New information gives new hope and reassigns the objection.

### Authority
- Authority's job is absolute conviction. It's the fastest way to build belief, and the brands that scale hardest have it. **Signal it before the pitch, not during.**
- **Levels:** you are the authority; you learned from one; you know of one. The authority doesn't have to appear in the ad.
- **Ten types:**
  - credential (MD, vet)
  - following (influencer)
  - media/press
  - awards/patents
  - organization (MIT, NFL, Navy SEALs)
  - title (founder, CEO; make founder videos)
  - research (studies, lab data, public stats)
  - experience ("mom of five," "I tried 37 pre-workouts")
  - borrowed ("Navy SEALs use cold exposure; we applied it," "researchers discovered," "experts say")
  - visual (podcast set, stage, a scar, a speaker who visibly has the result)
- **Two free enhancers:**
  - **Relatability:** describe their pain better than anyone.
  - **Transparency:** drawbacks, who it's not for, why it costs more ("It tastes awful. And it works.").
- Stack authority strategically. "My speech therapist recommended it" is night and day versus "I did research."

### Awareness
Name the starting stage and do its job, then carry the viewer through every stage to most aware.

| Stage | Job |
|---|---|
| Unaware | Identity hook → make them aware of the problem or desire → solutions → why this one → offer. Longest copy. |
| Problem aware | Dramatize the need until it's worth solving ("especially that left pinky"). Don't make them relive it too long. |
| Solution aware | Address the other options and why they fall short. |
| Product aware | Today's biggest segment. Beat "this category didn't work for me" with a new mechanism, reassignment and authority. |
| Most aware | A great offer, low risk, easy purchase. Get out of the way. |

- **True unaware hooks name no problem.** "The hidden reason you're exhausted" is problem aware, because it names exhaustion. Unaware is pure curiosity, education or entertainment ("You'll never guess what's floating in your air right now"). AI mislabels this constantly.
- Changing the comparison target moves the awareness level: your humidifier vs. another humidifier is product aware; your slide board vs. a Peloton is solution aware.
- "Are you tired?" isn't a strong problem-aware hook: tired people already know every solution, and on a day they aren't tired they think "not me."

### Sophistication
- Assume e-com markets are stage 3–5. They've heard every claim. You can have the right avatar, desire and product and still not be believed.
- **Belief levers:** a new mechanism (strongest), new information, or a new identity (a more specific, underserved avatar).
- **Check saturation before picking a claim.** Tally competitor claims in the ad library. ("Fast drying without heat damage" failed because seven competitors said it.)

### Beliefs
- Beliefs are as strong as desires. Violate one even slightly and nothing you promise matters. "Your most powerful claim isn't always your most powerful headline."
- Find beliefs by working backwards from behavior, experience and emotion in research. Log "people believe X."
- Fit their version of the facts and their logic. Show people with the same beliefs succeeding.
- **Over-claiming kills belief.** One justifiable promise beats a product that "does sleep, focus, joints, gut, wrinkles…"
- **Four belief tools:**
  - **Yes-train:** questions only the right person answers "yes" to.
  - **Symptoms:** hyper-detailed experience, so "this person gets it."
  - **Authority:** acknowledge the belief, then loosen it with strong evidence.
  - **Logic chain:** undeniable statements leading to the mechanism ("hot air dries faster → excess heat damages → you need the right heat → it auto-cycles temperature").

### The mini memo before writing
**Why** (the evidence) / **What** (the ad in one templated line) / **How** (a reference ad). This kills half-baked pitches like "let's do an us-vs-them."

## 4. Hooks

- **The hook is the writer's #1 job,** but its job is to stop the *right* person and keep them. A bite that falls off is worthless; hook and bridge are one unit.
- **Must-haves:** emotion, a curiosity gap, high stakes. Stakes can live in the visual.
- **Start in the action.** Zero backstory, zero chaos. "Curious, not confused." A confused brain scrolls.
- **Targeted vs. broad.** Targeted calls out the audience or a label (easier; the default for most). Broad lets anyone watch, then funnels down (more scalable, but needs elite copy).
- **Promise or open loop?** Ask "is the context itself interesting?" If yes, state it as a promise and deliver. If not (often the product), open a loop. ("California floats shade balls to battle drought": ~300K views. "Why are 96 million black balls on this reservoir?": ~96M views.)
- **Curiosity is shaped like a lowercase n.** It's weakest when they have no idea and weakest when they know exactly; strongest when they *think* they know but aren't sure. A good hook tells 80% and holds back 20%.
- **Three ways to open loops:**
  1. **Juxtaposition** (#1): two things that don't belong. "I quit the gym and lost 20 pounds," "14+ hours, no swelling," a tree photo + "My husband was on a waiting list to receive a heart." Works with text, image + copy, clip + copy, or the image alone.
  2. **Fear:** "Watch out for these ingredients…," "3 red flags of vitamin deficiency in kids." *(house)* Strong fear is allowed when the danger is real and the product answers it; the limit is the platform (principle 8), not intensity.
  3. **Do the opposite:** list what every competitor says and invert it ("Here's why my product sucks").
- **Signal a secret:** "Elite marathon runners size their shoes differently than the store recommends."
- **Unaware openers:** hidden fear, hidden desire, did-you-know, debate ("Who has a better life, cats or dogs?"). Lead with identity, not product, then story, then bridge to the solution. Unaware ads don't feel like ads at first, which is why they scale.
- **Flash-forward:** put the payoff in the hook, then explain how you got there.
- **Low-valence hooks** give people a reason to watch "to make sure nothing's wrong with them" ("3 signs your body's working against your diet").
- **Make it picturable and specific.** Name the specific thing; odd, specific numbers.
- **Bossy claims fail.** "Stop scrolling and watch this" gives no reason. "If you have X, give me 5 seconds" does.
- **Visual-only hooks** work when the visual is abnormal (eggs dropped on insoles). Ordinary visuals need voice or text.
- **Agree-first hooks** win with sensitive audiences: "Here's why gaining weight postpartum is normal" beat a market full of "lose 20 lbs in 4 weeks."

## 5. Bridges

- **Hook = stop the scroll. Bridge = keep them.** The bridge is where viewers decide to stay. If you can't bridge a hook, it's the wrong hook.
- **Write a custom bridge for every hook.** Hook + bridge ≈ 8 seconds per variation, which also helps Meta see the variants as different.
- **The six bridges:**
  1. **Introduce the mechanism**, then tie back to the benefit. ("The [mechanism] is what lets me [desire].")
  2. **Escalate the problem:** "I thought I was being a good [role]… but after [time] it was still happening." Show it getting worse.
  3. **Escalate the desire** to its dream extreme, or third-party recognition ("Nobody believes I'm 54 anymore").
  4. **Reposition the problem:** state the real cause, ideally via an authority. ("You don't need scratch mittens. You need him to stop scratching.")
  5. **New information:** "It turns out we've been using the wrong materials."
  6. **Timeline:** "It took me 30 years of doing laundry to learn this. I'll teach it to you in one minute."
- **Facts bridge better than opinions.** "Are you one of the 96% of dog owners who don't brush daily? By age three you could be setting your dog up for a lifetime of dental issues."
- **Reassurance kills urgency.** "It's actually extremely common" lost to "it's a yeast problem feeding on your scalp like a buffet. Every flake is proof it's alive."
- **Test the hookless cut (in media res).** Write the whole ad, delete the hook, and if it still makes sense starting at the mechanism or action, test it as a fourth variation. Their $1M+ and $2M+ spend ads started straight in the mechanism.

## 6. The body (the hold)

Everything after the bridge builds belief. Different viewers click at different points depending on how much belief they need.

### Default structure
Objection → claim → proof → benefit (as scenes) → secondary benefit → risk reversal → price/CTA. Cycle objection → claim → proof as needed.

Worked example (a ~45-second appetite-supplement hold):
1. **Specific objection:** "You've tried GLP-1 supplements, appetite suppressants and natural alternatives." Specific beats "you've tried supplements."
2. **Why they failed, as new information:** "They only fix half the problem. They boost your fullness hormone; none of them protect it."
3. **Escalation, with humor from real customer language:** "…and next thing you know you're elbow-deep in a bag of something crunchy."
4. **Unique mechanism:** "Except [product], the first collagen with [ingredient], clinically shown to block [enzyme]."
5. **Proof:** study details.
6. **Social proof:** *(house)* a believable number; it may be dramatized, never a fake review attributed to a real, named customer or outlet.
7. **Future pacing:** "Imagine going hours without food crossing your mind." Then tie back: "That's what happens when you *protect* your hormone, not just boost it."
8. **Secondary benefit.**
9. **Offer in a third-person "reporter" voice:** "This company is so confident you'll love it that you can try it 60 days risk-free."
10. **Enemy close + price per day:** "Your body's been fighting you. This helps you fight back. Less than $2 a day."

### Objections: prevent first, then handle
- The best handling is prevention: copy so good the objection never forms. Keep an objection list from research, and turn each one into "how do we show…?"
- **Three types:** product/market ("this category doesn't work"), claim ("too good to be true"), avatar ("not for someone like me").
- **Default objection by sophistication:** stage 1–2 "does it work?" (proof, mechanism, authority); stage 3–4 "how is yours different?" (new mechanism + authority, and their past failures weren't their fault); stage 5 "this isn't for me" (identity, positioning).
- **Five techniques, strongest first:**
  1. **Facts that imply the claim:** "sold out 43 times," not "delicious cookies." *(house)* Scarcity and harmless stats may be dramatized (principle 6); never a checkable false claim about a real person or institution.
  2. **Reversal:** make the objection the selling point ("Kids know there's greens in the gummies and they still love them"; "Lose weight even if you cheat").
  3. **"Because":** a reason makes a claim believable ("easiest humidifier to clean *because* it's stainless steel, so it goes in the dishwasher").
  4. **Reassign the objection:** agree, then move the blame to a specific target inside the category ("Most probiotics die in stomach acid; these are delayed-release"). It gives permission to try again.
  5. **Gradualization:** a stream of acceptances. Promise → proof → promise; reuse an accepted sentence structure to carry a new claim; "this expert found… this expert found…" so the narrator only relays.

  Stream-of-acceptances example: "If you have a kid, you probably have a humidifier in their room. And if you have a humidifier, you probably hate cleaning it every few days. I've gone through about three brands…"
- **Price: never defend it, reframe it.** Per-day breakdown ("$1.97 a day, less than a bottle of kombucha"); compare up ("transplants cost $15,000"); reframe the desire ("you're not buying a powder, you're buying the ability to wear the clothes in the back of your closet").

### Raise the stakes (after drafting)
Ask "how can more be at stake?" Each lever works positive or negative:
1. **Bigger:** "my dog peed" → "my dog ruined my $10,000 hardwood floors."
2. **A witness:** "…in front of our landlord."
3. **Urgent:** "Every day you delay is another day the teeth get more yellow."
4. **Permanent:** "a lifetime of dental problems."
5. **Costs more:** money, time, relationships ("your wife will start sleeping in another room").

### Storytelling
- **Most buyers are high-information.** They need more information to buy today, and stories make information interesting. They also lower defenses, because brand-delivered facts carry an obvious agenda.
- **Your job isn't to convince them; it's to get them to convince themselves.** Viewers project into the character. The prospect has to see themselves in the story.
- **Story = conflict + change.** "The man worked at his desk" → "…at the *boss's* desk."
- **Five building blocks:**
  1. **Change:** something changed, or is about to.
  2. **Withheld information:** storytelling is the art of withholding.
  3. **Stakes and drama:** people care about the abnormal.
  4. **Tension and suspense:** unresolved, resolved later. Link beats with **but** and **so**, never **and**; "and, and, and" is a shopping list.
  5. **Cause and effect:** every line leads to the next, so viewers insert their own memories.
- **Laws:**
  - No people, no story.
  - No ordinary, no story: small made big beats big made small ("send us a few old shirts" beats "millions of shirts turned into blankets").
  - If you start explaining, there's no story: delete the explanation and give an example.
- **Blame the industry, not the customer.** Make the customer feel good about themselves and they'll trust you.
- **Mine stories from reviews,** but never paste a review straight onto an ad. Find the interesting fragment and build a plot ("gave me my garage back" → "Rats took over my garage. Here's how a vacuum took it back").
- **Story prompts:**
  - What does everyone believe about your product that isn't true?
  - What would frustrate people about it?
  - What made you angry enough to create it?
  - What do competitors hope customers never figure out?
  - Who is it NOT for?
- **Arcs are iteration levers, not formulas:**
  - **Overcoming the monster:** problem → failed fixes → this worked.
  - **Rags to riches.**
  - **In media res:** start in the action or the dream outcome.
  - **Quest:** aspirational.
  - **Voyage and return:** "I tried the extreme so you don't have to." Good for skeptics.
  - **Rebirth:** good for unaware audiences who've accepted the status quo.
  - **Confusion:** good for sophisticated markets.
  - **Two paths:** the bad future vs. the product path.
  - **Tragedy** *(house)*: a pure tragedy with no way out drives awareness, not conversion. As the warning (the one who didn't have it), followed by the product path and the regret beat, it converts; Palace Guard runs it ("we never found her").

### Enemies and positioning
- **Every story ad has an enemy.** Find it among Approaches (a different way of solving it), Beliefs (what you stand for or against) and Competitors (needs a real "why we're better").
- **Be different, not "better."** Saying "better" invites disbelief; "different" makes them conclude "better" on their own. Visual difference + message difference is powerful.
- **Be first.** If you can't own a category, own a subcategory.
- **Reposition what you're up against:** contrast, not comparison ("for people who can't take aspirin").
- **Name a strategic enemy.** Contrasting against an accepted belief is easier than changing a held one.
- **Say no to something** (no added sugar, one size, closed Sundays).
- **Look for the hole:** what competitors won't say, show or do. ("280 calories per pint" told people to eat the whole pint.)
- A breakthrough with no differentiation gets copied fast.

## 7. The pitch and CTA

- **Deliver the payoff, then establish belief, then pitch.** If you promised four reasons, give four. Pitch before belief and they click away.
- **Pitch as a recommendation, not a sale:**
  - "My [authority] recommended I try this."
  - "If you want the one I'm using, it's from [brand]."
  - "I bought three of these; the one from [brand] is the best."

  "I did research and found…" doesn't land.
- **Offer as generosity or with a reason:** "Our best bundle ever. It's hurting our margins but we don't care" beat "save up to 25%." Vulnerability builds trust.
- **Risk reversal** turns a premium price into "I can just return it." Name the guarantee specifically. Honest timelines in guarantees for sensitive audiences ("because real results take time").
- **Great payoffs drive shares:** "I got value," "I discovered something new," "I know someone who needs this."

## 8. Statics

- **Image ads are easy to make and hard to make good.** You have one box and no time to explain. The easier the format, the better the copy has to be. Primary text matters.
- **Templates work because of their mechanism, not their look.** Pick one whose mechanism fits your angle, fill it with the sub-avatar's own language (never the template's headline), make the image congruent with the headline, write strong primary text. If it fails, blame the copy before the format. The same template flopped for one brand and scaled to $15K/day for another a month later.
- **Template mechanisms that win:**
  - avatar call-out + a real review ("moms of 15-year-olds")
  - solution exaggeration with a bold number ("14+ hours, no swelling")
  - stealing credibility (borrow a known mechanism or authority)
  - "this is not the product" ("This is a meal, not a protein shake")
  - specific pain point ("Lose the muffin top for good," not "lose weight")
  - accidental ideal outcome ("I accidentally lost 12 lbs at 52")
  - indirect avatar ("Teen always grumpy?" speaks to the parent)
  - simplified us-vs-them ("This is bread. This is breakfast.")
  - minimal us-vs-them (highlight what yours *doesn't* have)
  - native style (never fake press)
  - offer ads for most-aware buyers
- **Image-first method:**
  1. Find a template outside your niche.
  2. Give AI the screenshot plus product info and verbatim sub-avatar research.
  3. Ask for headlines and checkmark bullets per sub-avatar that mirror its structure at a 5th-grade level.
  4. Keep the ones that name a specific behavior or internal monologue.
- **Lead the bullets with a fact.** Back every claim on the ad itself ("Cleanest portable protein" + "Ingredients: cod, salt. That's it.").
- **Ugly ads are fine if the information is great.** The same template with different headlines is still a big enough swing if each targets different people.
- **Per-sub-avatar rewrites beat product-first copy.** A canned fish brand: "You stopped being hungry. Your muscles didn't." (GLP-1 users); "Tuna macros. No tuna smell." (high performers).

## 9. Video scripts

- **Structure:** 3 hooks, each with its own matched bridge (~8 seconds each), then one universal hold (objection → claim → proof → benefit → secondary benefit → risk reversal → price/CTA). About 43–45 seconds for most direct ads; VSLs (2–4+ minutes) when you need deeper education, proof and objection handling, without fluff.
- **Brief it line by line:** hook + bridge per variation, then the hold with each line labeled by its job, so every line gets thought through. Script first, visuals later. Hooks are non-negotiable to brief scene by scene; bridges are worth it too.
- **Polishing:**
  - Replace generic objections with specific ones from research.
  - Choose terms from customer language and keep them consistent.
  - "Scientists call it X" keeps authority without a first-person claim.
  - Use a third-person reporter voice for the offer.
  - Add comic relief from real customer quotes.
- **Sound and look are part of the copy.** Music and audio set valence and intensity (dark piano builds dread). Editing style must fit the demographic. Subtitles + B-roll with no voiceover makes viewers read in their own inner voice: they sell it to themselves.
- **Delay the product until belief exists.** One near-winner died because the product arrived halfway, before the viewer believed the problem, and the unique mechanism was never named. The rewrite doubled the length, moved the product to ~1:10, named the mechanism, led with an agree-first hook and a simple analogy ("Prolactin is screaming 'eat everything.' GLP-1 helps you say no."), and won.

## 10. Working with AI

- **AI is a predictive model.** Pure AI copy is guaranteed to sound like everyone else. It gets you 70–80% of the way; your last 20–30% (real research, customer language, line edits, sometimes one or two words in a hook) decides winners.
- **Context beats prompting.** Give it real customer quotes, Reddit threads, the product doc, the previous script's results and learnings, the hypothesis, and **your own diagnosis and intent.**
- **Give specific, reasoned feedback:** "more headlines like this one, because it shows the internal monologue." Voice-to-text is faster.
- **Correct it.** It mislabels awareness (calling problem-aware hooks unaware), invents or overstates facts, names mechanisms the product doesn't have, and copies inspiration ads too literally ("three nutrient deficiencies" became "three hormone imbalances"). Fact-check claims about the product, the mechanism and real people or institutions; a dramatized fear-side stat is allowed (principle 6), so don't flag it.
- **Use it to research customer language:** "What do customers actually call this?" ("fullness hormone," not "I'm-full hormone"). Ask for source links and read them yourself.
- **Never one-shot a script.** Take the output into your brief and read it line by line as the customer. Is the problem clear? Is the reveal convincing? Is the bridge smooth? Then go back for targeted fixes. "Be a marketer, not an AI regurgitator."

## 11. Diagnosing and iterating

### 13 reasons ads fail
1. No emotional impact: it doesn't sound like the avatar's internal conversation.
2. No curiosity.
3. No suspense or payoff.
4. Too complex (over grade 6).
5. Empty claims ("best," "better than," with no proof).
6. Bossy claims ("stop scrolling").
7. The same claim competitors make, said the same way.
8. Written, not spoken.
9. Written for yourself, not in the customer's words.
10. Angle too broad (template headline copied verbatim).
11. Angle everyone already says.
12. Editing style doesn't fit the demographic.
13. The wrong format. A failed static doesn't prove the angle failed.

### Angle or execution?
- Test one angle across several formats. All fail: likely the angle. Any one shows direction: it's execution, so keep communicating the angle better.
- Most "dead angles" are execution failures. Give an angle up to ~3 batches, each with new evidence of what was wrong.

### By outcome
- **Loser:** usually the hook (not relatable, an empty claim, wrong valence or intensity). Other causes: the visuals don't tell the platform whom to serve; the TAM is too niche or too broad for your skill; an opinion bridge; no authority; the offer.
- **Spend winner** (gets spend, misses KPI): rarely a click problem. Add trust (authority or vulnerability: "depending on how bad it is, this *may still* help"), real urgency ("every day you delay…"), bigger stakes, or a congruent landing page. Check the hook isn't attracting attention that won't buy. Fix the cause before re-formatting.
- **Breakthrough:** study it, iterate on it, and don't kill it over a few bad days.
- **Iteration levers:**
  - **Component rewrite (biggest lever):** keep what works, re-execute it differently (intensify the authority, shift the desire from health to status, change the awareness level).
  - **Hook valence/intensity:** flip positive/negative, turn intensity up or down.
  - **Bridge remake** with more authority, urgency and stakes.
  - **In media res cut.**
  - **Extend the problem section** (compounding problems).
  - **Handle an objection from the comments** (comment-reply hooks).
  - **Negative framing:** "Honestly, don't buy this, here's why," then disprove each point.
  - **New sub-avatar.**
  - **New format:** voiceover ↔ text on screen, founder or authority delivery, podcast, animated ingredient hero vs. villain, talking body parts, a song, a mega-ad stitching winning clips.
- **Learnings are hypotheses, not facts.** They become more fact-like only when an iteration built on them wins. Write them customer-first ("which sub-avatar, why they cared"), not structural ("it was a video"), and always leave with an action plan.

### Steal frameworks, not ads
Chunk a winning hook up to its emotional mechanic: "I think I just got scammed" → "I think [negative thing] just happened" → "I just found out most of these companies are lying to you." Borrow across categories. Test a 1:1 control plus two reinterpretations.

## 12. Ethics and compliance *(house)*

The house line replaces this course's section (CLAUDE.md principle 6, Reed 2026-11-12).
- **The moral line is the value exchange, not the persuasion.** If she has the problem, the product solves it, and the outcome promised is one it delivers, then what gets her to buy is fair: dramatized mechanisms and stories, invented personas, fabricated-but-harmless stats, fake scarcity, absolutes, strong fear.
- **Out, always:** anything that would get someone hurt if they believed it; a capability the product can't deliver when it matters; a worded hundred-percent guarantee; a checkable false claim about a real person or institution; a mechanism the product doesn't have.
- **Platform rules are a separate axis** (principle 8): the word rule, the image rule, and each brand's own landmines in its `brand.md`. Say a platform risk once, labeled, and it's Reed's call.
- **Borrowed authority:** "experts say," "researchers found" are fine; never imply a real named person or institution endorses the product.

## 13. Checklists

### Before writing
1. Who is this for, and why will they care (desire + belief)?
2. One angle: the main reason to buy.
3. Which awareness stage does it start at, and what job does that stage need?
4. What mechanism proves the claim? New, unspoken or renamed, and useful?
5. What authority, and does it come before the pitch?
6. Why / What / How memo.

### Hook and bridge
7. Targeted or broad?
8. Promise or open loop (is the context interesting)?
9. Emotion, curiosity gap, high stakes (in copy or visual)?
10. Juxtaposition, ethical fear, or do the opposite?
11. Starts in the action, zero backstory, zero chaos?
12. Written in the environment, line breaks checked?
13. Which of the six bridges, customized to this hook?
14. Hookless cut tested as a variation?

### Body
15. Every line passes the three checks (visual, falsifiable, ownable)? Survives a competitor swap?
16. Facts over opinions?
17. Shown, not told?
18. Objections named and handled?
19. Stakes raised with at least one lever?
20. Customer language and labels, not copywriter words?
21. Communicates the intended feeling?
22. Delete pass done, grade 6 or lower, reads naturally out loud?

### Pitch
23. Payoff delivered and belief established before the CTA?
24. Pitched as a recommendation? Price reframed, not defended?

### Pre-launch gut check
- Do the first 1–2 seconds hook the *right* person and keep them?
- Would the customer *want* to watch this?
- Does the copy paint a clear picture?
- Does it feel native to the platform, and not look like an ad?
- Is there a label?
- Is it relatable?
- Would it make someone smile or laugh?
- Does it feel authentic?
- Which learnings from past winners did it apply?
- Does it educate creatively on the core desire?
- Is there a story they're invested in?
- Is there playful escalation ("If Mr. Clean invented a humidifier")?
- Does it separate you from competitors?
- Is the visual congruent with the headline, and the landing page congruent with the ad?
