## What a Movie is here
Same job as every ad: install the one belief (her daughter could be assaulted at any moment, and a personal alarm is how you stop it) and get the parent to buy. A movie does it with scenes the viewer watches instead of a story she's told. Length is never the problem; a boring scene is (principle 1). The winners hold 6 to 14 minutes because every scene either hurts, explains, or pays off.

## The skeleton (all four winners)
1. **Cold open = the wound, in the scene, in the first line of dialogue. Nobody talks to the camera (Reed, RP5, 2026-11-09: "It is a movie, they don't talk to the viewers.").** Granny pats her stomach: "When are you due?" The guys in the diner: "That's Bear's wife." The hook is said by one character to another, and the viewer understands it on one listen. No narrator line, no V.O. over the hook, not even one sentence. The keyword is spoken by whoever would really say it: the officer on the phone, the witness calling 911, the detective at the table (hooks.md still rules: predator, abducted, assaulted, never a stand-in). **But the character's own words win over the keyword (Reed, RP9, 2026-11-13):** he swapped my "Santa abducted her grandkids" and "kids being abducted" for what those people would actually yell ("I think Santa took them!" · "I found the missing kids! The Santa was in on it!"), so in a movie the keyword goes in only where that character would really say it. The plate carries the rest. For us it's a flash-forward to a moment in the body.
2. **The wound happens in public.** Someone else does it, out loud, with witnesses: the whole bleacher section boos; the mean friend says it in her ear; the club knew for months. For us: the manager ten feet away, the lot full of people, the store that knew for two weeks.
3. **The private low point, mostly silent.** Alone at the truck, the bathroom mirror, the car in the driveway with the engine off. One or two V.O. lines over held shots of a face. This is where the lead says the one line that names the identity the wound broke ("I was the pretty one my whole life." · "I used to be the scariest man in that club.").
4. **The expert, in a long dialogue scene.** A friend who used to have it, a guy at the gym, a boxing coach, a 92-year-old father. Always someone who lived it, never a salesman. This is the longest scene in every winner (two to three minutes) and the only place long speeches are allowed. Ours: the detective at the kitchen table.
**The expert never tells the lead what she saw herself (Reed, RP10: the detective pointing out there were no cables, "she would obviously know that").** He tells her what she couldn't see: who he really is, the second car, why he picked her, why it works.
5. **Objections are spoken by the lead and answered in the scene.** "I cut the wine, I did keto." · "My doctor says it's normal." · "Drops? You're messing with me." · "I know the physiology." The expert knocks each one down. The viewer's own doubts come out of the lead's mouth, so they get answered before she has them. Ours: "She broke her brother's nose. Why couldn't she just scream?"
6. **The expert takes the blame off.** "You're not weak, brother. You're backed up." · "You don't got a weight problem. You got sludge." Ours: "Freezing isn't weakness. It happens to almost every girl, the tough ones too."
7. **The mechanism gets a cutaway or a screen.** The winners cut to a 3D animation of the clogged drain. Ours is the parking lot footage on the detective's laptop: no sound, every head turns.
8. **The product changes hands on camera.** Dani walks to her car and comes back with the bottle and closes Ashley's fingers over it. The bottle alone on the locker room bench. The dose is said out loud in the scene. Ours: the alarm clipped on her keys (planted early), the pin, the strobe.
9. **The rival gets named by the expert, never by the narrator.** "Don't grab whatever pops up online, most of them are weak doses and fillers." · "Most companies sell pale yellow garlic oil and call it aged garlic." Ours: "the cheap colorful ones in evidence bags."
10. **A dip before the climb.** The person who should believe doesn't: the husband on his phone ("Ash, you've tried everything"), the wife ("You've said that before"). The lead answers with a deadline ("Give me six weeks").
11. **Proof over time, through other people, mostly silent, with title cards.** "5 WEEKS OUT" · "WEEK 2" · "Two months later." A coworker, the tailor ("we need to take it in"), a club brother ("you dropping weight, Bear?"). The lead never says it works; other people notice.
12. **The payoff happens in the same kind of place as the wound, with the same people.** The next wedding, the same mean friend. The same bleacher guys at the gas station. Cody at the rally. The patient who refused the pills comes back. The line that wounded comes back and lands the other way ("Careful, Barbie, somebody's going to ask when you're due." "Let him ask.").
13. **The close is the lead's V.O. over product shots:** the story in one sentence aimed at the viewer ("If you were always the pretty one and somewhere along the way you got puffy…"), the mechanism in one sentence, the dose, the offer, the guarantee, "if the button's still there, grab it." Ours adds the "Maybe" lines (song.md), the regret beat, the share CTA, and "Don't wait."

## Perspective
**It's a movie: the story is told by people talking to each other, never to the viewer, until the close.** The lead is on screen and is the one the movie is about. Mid-story V.O. is only a short thought in the moment, in the lead's head ("Weird. But whatever." · "Something was moving. I didn't tell anybody."), never narration that explains or sets up ("you need to know who I am first" is out). Anything a narrator would explain becomes a scene or a line between characters: her toughness is her brother on the floor yelling "she broke my nose"; the freeze is her answer when the officer asks "Did you scream?"; the mother's guilt is said to the daughter. **The close is the only place the lead speaks to the viewer**, in V.O. over product shots, the way all four winners end.
On RP5 Reed called for the daughter's side, so Paige is the lead and the close is hers; the mother's regret and the product facts come out in scenes (Dana at the detective's table). **The close is the daughter's V.O., talking to the mother watching (Reed, RP5 and again RP10, 2026-11-15: I gave RP10's close to the mother and Reed moved it to the daughter).** **She describes herself as the buyer's daughter, so the mother watching thinks "that's just like my daughter," then lands on better safe than sorry (Reed, RP10: the "Maybe your daughter…" lines "don't really work").** **She's independent, never a mommy's girl (Reed, RP11, 2026-11-15: a close where she texts Mom from the car and rolled her eyes "reads too much like a daughter who has a helicopter mom").** The mother watching worries about a grown daughter living her own life; she can't always be there, but she can protect her. So the daughter shows her own life (her rent, her shift, her car), owns the mistake herself, says her mom couldn't be there, and confirms the alarm kept her safe when she couldn't. **Every trait is a moment from this movie, not a general one (Reed, RP10: generic traits "feel a little disconnected from the rest of the story"):** "I blush and give him my Instagram," "I laughed, because I didn't want to be rude," "I told her he was too hot to abduct people," "when he asked me to grab his cables, I almost did," over the scene the story was heading to (her brother blowing out the candles from the Target bag); then the detective's line that she's exactly the girl he looks for; then "my mom bought it because she'd rather be safe than sorry"; then the offer ("my mom bought three") and "if your daughter sounds anything like me, please get her one."

## The shape (in the script file)
```
# RP## — <working title>
Persona: daughter (narrator) · Awareness: UA · Format: Movie
Status: drafting
Parent: — · Lever: —
Cast: Paige (16, narrates) · Dana (her mom, 40s) · the detective (50s, flat and quiet)

## V1
### Script
[SCENE 1 — the kitchen at home, just after sunset]
(Dana's phone on the counter shows Paige's dot sitting in the Target parking lot)
Dana: Hey, did you get the eggs?
Officer (on the phone): Ma'am, your daughter's safe. Three men just tried to abduct her in the Target parking lot.
(Dana slides down the cabinet to the kitchen floor)
```
- `[SCENE n — place, time]` opens each scene. `(…)` is what the camera sees, one visual per line (principle 2), so a silent beat is its own line: `(she looks at the manager)` · `(she opens her mouth, then closes it)`. `Name: line` is speech. `(V.O.)` is voice-over, `(O.S.)` off screen, `(on the phone)` for a voice on a call.
- **Silence is written, not implied.** The winners run 15 to 30 second stretches with no words: the montage, the low point, the payoff walk-in. Write each shot as its own direction line so the editor knows the beat is there.
- **The variations change only the cold open, and the file is laid out the way the editor cuts it (Reed, RP9, 2026-11-13):** every `## V#` block first, each holding only its hook and ending on where the body picks up, then one `## Body` / `### Script` block. Never "then V1 from SCENE 2": it reads as V1's hook. **The hook is `[HOOK — place, time]`, not a scene, and the body's numbering starts at SCENE 1 (Reed, RP10, 2026-11-14: "i count scene 1 as the start of the body, with v1,v2,v3 as each hook").** A hook that is the body's own first lines says which scene it replaces (RP10 V2: "Scene 1 isn't used"). Not every opening has to be a flash-forward: RP9 runs a radio-in-the-car start, a normal start, the parent's side, and an action start.
- **No Text Hook plate on a movie** (Reed cut them on RP5): the cold open carries the hook alone.
- **Each variation is named for its job** (Reed, RP5): `## V1 - Action start` · `## V2 - Moms POV` · `## V3 - Another action start`. Pick the jobs before writing the hooks. **Open in drama or curiosity, not in the action (Reed, RP10, 2026-11-14: "variations work better when starting in drama, or curiosity, not in immediate action")**: a flash-forward to the aftermath (the hospital bed, the call), a scene that asks a question (the footage, "he hasn't bought a thing"), the mother's side. RP5's action starts are the older read. **And the hook opens a loop it doesn't close (Reed, RP10): show that something's wrong, never what he was there for or how it ends.** **A hook doesn't have to cut to black (Reed, RP10, 2026-11-15: "some of the hooks can pick up in the middle of the scene, without fading to black").** "In the scene" means in that moment in time, the story's own present, starting mid-moment (Reed, RP10: a flash-forward that pushes into a laptop and becomes the live scene "doesn't work"). **Every hook has to make sense going into the body (Reed, RP10, 2026-11-15: hooks that replayed a body scene in different words "make no sense"). Two shapes only:** a flash-forward that ends on a time card ("that afternoon") and then the body from SCENE 1; or the body's own first lines, entered in the moment, with the body continuing from the next line and the skipped scene named as unused. Never a reworded copy of a body scene. The hook starts mid-conversation in the story's present and the body picks up at a named line, or at the next scene: `(no cut; the body picks up in V1 SCENE 2 at "…" and runs to the end)`.
- **After the cold open, go straight into the day it happened** (Reed cut RP5's backstory scenes and the weeks-after montage). Who she is comes out in a line in the scene ("She's the most vocal girl on the block"), not a flashback; the proof is the event itself, so no proof montage unless Reed asks.

## Writing it
0. **When the danger is a person she likes, the front plays like a rom com (Reed, RP10, 2026-11-15: "much less obvious whats going on… slight comments here and there in conversation are what tip").** The tip lives in a line he says lightly ("you know those don't really work, right?" · "please tell me your boyfriend's not about to come around that corner"), never in an ominous camera direction (the kid staring again, the man not smiling anymore). One silent tip from Reed's outline is fine; three stacked tells the viewer the twist. **Plant each tell once, and nobody reacts to it (Reed, RP10: "I almost feel like im stacking too much detail"; the "since noon" tell planted four times made her look blind, not slightly wrong).** The strongest tell is a pattern the viewer doesn't see until the expert names it: every flirt question was him casing her ("Think about what he asked you… None of that was flirting."). The whole flow is "a rom com gone bad, but still a very realistic scenario" (Reed, RP10): every step of the con has to be something a real man could pull off in a real parking lot, judged by what the viewer believes, not by the fact. Flag only what she'd find fake on one watch. **And grounded beats extra (Reed, RP10, 2026-11-15): he cut the chloroform ("a little extra") for her noticing a woman hiding in his back seat, pulling the pin, bystanders running over ("what's going on over here?"), and fainting from the adrenaline; he made the predator 26, not 30 ("a slight misjudgement by the daughter, which is totally realistic"); and he moved her to college, where her mom calls to check on her.**
1. **Short lines, people talking past each other.** Three to ten words is normal in a scene; interruptions and half-answers are how people talk. Only the expert gets runs of several sentences. Nobody tells someone what both already know.
2. **Every speaker gets a register, and the persona registers still hold.** The daughter rationalizes before she worries ("Weird. But whatever."); the mother is cautious, not dumb, never controlling; the detective is flat and quiet on the darkest lines and says "look." A new character gets three words they'd say and one they never would, decided before their first line.
3. **Plant the product early, pay it off late.** The winners hand it over mid-story; ours is already on her keys, so show it going on (the August driveway, "what if it goes off in class?") before the danger, and call that line back after ("Because it doesn't go off in class.").
4. **Dramatic irony, cut in.** The viewer sees where the parent was while it happened: the dot on the phone, the oven preheating. An intercut scene, not a sentence after.
5. **The mechanism is on screen and matches the hardware:** a keychain, a pin, 130 dB, the strobe. A viewer believes what she sees over what she's told.
6. **Roller coaster across scenes.** A two-minute kitchen table, then four three-second shots. Three scenes of the same length or mood in a row is a rewrite (principle 7).
7. **Profanity:** the winners curse constantly ("Normal, my ass," "didn't do shit"). Ours doesn't until Reed says so; the buyer is a woman 45 to 70.
7a. **The threat is a trend, never one man (Reed, RP11, 2026-11-15: a detective hook about one serial predator "makes it seem like one person, which means it feels less likely to happen to the viewer").** The movie informs her that this happens, everywhere, so she prepares: different men, different cars, the same trick; the news says "more than one man"; the expert says they get creative and move on to the next trick, so the payoff is being prepared, not dodging this one trick. One caught man is fine if the expert says he's not the only one.
8. **The word rule covers everything:** speech, directions and plates. Never the banned words; "assaulted" and "abducted" are the ceiling; death is implied ("I can't talk about her," "only two came home") (principle 8).
9. **The image rule is now in the script.** Directions are the visuals, so nothing in a `(…)` line shows or suggests a sexual threat: no girl pinned, no hand over a mouth in close-up, no drink being spiked, no weapon in frame. Men walking toward her, the open van door, her face: the threat lives in distance and in what she can't do.

## Before handing it back
- The scanner runs on every edit in Movie mode: banned words fail anywhere (speech or direction), symbols and ALL CAPS fail in speech, fragment and repeated-shape checks are off. Plate text shows up as "(unlabeled)" words; that's expected.
- Read every V.O. line before the close: each one is a thought in the moment, never a sentence to the viewer. If one explains or sets up, turn it into a scene or a line between characters.
- Read the cast list against the skeleton: who wounds, who explains, who doubts, who notices. A movie missing one of those is missing a beat.
