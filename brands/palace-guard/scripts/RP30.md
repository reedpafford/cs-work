It was 2015 in Juárez, a factory town just outside Monterrey, and most of the men there worked the night shift, so after dark the whole neighborhood was women and kids.
That’s when he started.
He wore a baseball cap and carried a knife, and he came in across the roofs, so the first thing a woman heard was footsteps right over her bed.
Then the back window.
Lucero was a single mom, asleep in her own house. He brutally assaulted her and left her there on her bedroom floor.
When she told people what happened, other women started talking. He’d been in their houses too, some of them with their kids asleep in the next room, and most of them had never told a soul, because they didn’t think anybody would ever catch him.
Women pushed dressers in front of their windows, and mothers slept in the same bed as their daughters, and he still kept finding a way in.
So the women decided they’d catch him themselves.
Initially, sixty neighbors signed up, and by the end there were a hundred and thirty. Mostly moms and daughters.
Every night each woman took a few blocks and watched them until the sun came up. A woman named Sandra took midnight to two in the morning, every night, for more than a year.
They didn’t have smartphones, so they used whistles.
One whistle meant a stranger was on the street. Two meant keep your eyes open. Three meant drop everything and run.
They sharpened their husbands’ machetes and grabbed baseball bats too, but that’s not what caught him.
In September of 2016, a woman on watch saw a man jump off a roof and duck behind a taco stand.
She blew three times.
Women came running from every block, in their slippers, with their flashlights, and they had him surrounded before he could get back up.
Lucero was the first one to point at him. Eighteen women identified him after that, and according to local media, he confessed to over ten attacks.
One of the neighbors said that was the day people stopped being afraid.
He got away with it for a year and a half, and three blows of a whistle ended his reign of terror.
