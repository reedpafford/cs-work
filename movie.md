## What a Movie is here
Several speakers, scenes, and a run time well past a talking head. Same job as every ad: install the one belief (her daughter could be assaulted at any moment, and a personal alarm is how you stop it) and get the parent to buy. Length is never the problem; a boring scene is (principle 1). A scene that doesn't move the belief is cut, however good it is.

## The shape (in the script file)
```
# RP## — <working title>
Persona: mother (narrator) · Awareness: UA · Format: Movie
Status: drafting
Parent: — · Lever: —
Cast: Harper (16, the daughter) · Mom (Karen, 52, narrates) · Officer Diaz (county detective)

## V1
### Text Hook
<the plate>
### Script
[SCENE 1 — corn maze, night]
(Harper walks ahead of her friends, phone flashlight on, laughing)
Harper: Guys, wait up, I can't see anything.
Mom (V.O.): Keep in mind, this was a family corn maze. There were strollers in there.
```
- `[SCENE n — place, time]` opens each scene. `(…)` is direction: what the camera sees, one visual per line (principle 2). `Name: line` is speech. `(V.O.)` is voice-over, `(O.S.)` off screen.
- V2 and V3 change the cold open (the hook scene) and keep the rest, the same as a talking head changes the hook line.

## Writing it
1. **One narrator carries the sell.** The persona from CLAUDE.md (the mother, the detective, the grandmother) owns the voice-over and the back half: the product block, the offer, the regret beat, the share CTA and "Don't wait" come from `write.md` in that persona's mouth, adapted, never split across characters.
2. **Every speaker gets a register, and the persona registers still hold.** The daughter rationalizes before she worries ("Weird, but whatever."); the mother is cautious, not dumb, never controlling; the detective is flat and quiet on the darkest lines. A new character gets three words they'd say and one they never would, written in the Cast line's head before their first line.
3. **Dialogue is people talking past each other, not explaining.** Nobody tells someone what both already know. Short lines, interruptions and half-answers are normal in dialogue, so the fragment budget is for the narrator, not for an exchange. The real-speech toolkit (principle 3) applies per speaker.
4. **The hook is the cold open.** The first five seconds are one scene with the keyword in it, said or on screen (hooks.md: comprehension first). No title card, no setup scene before the danger is visible or named.
5. **The mechanism is on screen.** The alarm, the pull, the sound and the reaction happen in a direction line, matching the real hardware (a keychain, a pin, 130 dB), because a viewer believes what she sees over what she's told.
6. **Roller coaster across scenes, not just lines.** Vary scene length and temperature: a long warm scene, then a ten-second cold one. Three scenes of the same length or mood in a row is a rewrite (principle 7).
7. **The word rule covers everything:** speech, directions and plates. Never the banned words; "assaulted" and "abducted" are the ceiling; death is implied (principle 8).
8. **The image rule is now in the script.** Directions are the visuals, so nothing written in a `(…)` line shows or suggests a sexual threat: no girl pinned, no hand over a mouth in close-up, no drink being spiked, no weapon in frame. Show the before and the after; the threat lives in the dialogue and the voice-over.

## Before handing it back
- The scanner runs on every edit in Movie mode: banned words fail anywhere in the script (speech or direction), symbols and ALL CAPS fail in speech, fragment and repeated-shape checks are off (dialogue is short by nature).
- Read the narrator's lines alone, top to bottom: they should make sense as a talking-head script on their own. If they don't, the sell is leaning on a scene the viewer might scroll past.
