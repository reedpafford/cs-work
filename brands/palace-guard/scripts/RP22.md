## Memo
Why: The Life Outline's "Your Life as Every Mexican Cartel Rank" (7.5M views, 10 minutes, hand-drawn) holds ten minutes on curiosity and insider economics alone, with no product; nobody in our category has put the mom inside the trafficker's head, and RP19 proved the insider picture sells. · What: your life at every rank of a human trafficker, from the kid on the mall bench to the man running six motels, and the girl who ended all of it. · How: the inspo's skeleton level for level (the rank, his age, the job, the pay, one incident, the rule he learns), with RP19's mechanism as the rule he learns over and over on the way up; the inspo's prison level becomes the close, where he realizes what beat him, and its successor level becomes the next kid on the bench.
Avatar: mothers and grandmothers 45 to 70 of a daughter in high school or college · Sub-avatar: the mom who scrolls past every safety ad but stops for a true-crime, how-it-really-works video.
Desire: her daughter is the girl he passes over.
Angle: he isn't a monster in a van, he's a guy with a job and rules, and every rule he learns is about staying unseen, so the one thing that ends his career is a girl who makes a scene.
Mechanism: attention. A girl is worth thousands a week only if nobody saw him take her; if she makes a scene, he lets her go, because there's always a quieter one. He planned for everything except the noise.
Authority: the narrator, and the trafficker's own realization in his cell (the enemy's confession; he finds out what beat him, he never recommends it).
Route: a new format, every rank, in hand-drawn animation like the inspo; voice from RP19; "you" stays the trafficker to the end, the narrator steps out for one CTA line. No ages for the girls, his ages only.

## Body
### Script
(hand-drawn animation like the inspo, a title card for each level; never a girl being taken on screen, only him, his phones, the car, the money and empty rooms)

Level one, the spotter.
You’re nineteen, and you sit on a bench in the food court at the mall with a phone and a lemonade, and you’ve got one job, which is to watch.
A girl sitting by herself with her earbuds in, you send a picture. A girl who says sorry when somebody bumps into her, you send a picture.
You get fifty dollars for every girl they use. Cash, and nobody asks you anything.
Nobody talks to you either. To them you’re part of the furniture, but you’re learning how it all works.
Mall security walks past you twice a day and doesn’t even look. Why would they? You’re just a kid on his phone.
One Saturday you send a picture of a girl by the pretzel place, and she turns around and looks right at you, and then she takes a picture of you.
That night a man you’ve never met sits down next to you. He doesn’t yell. He just says, never the one who draws attention.
You pick the quiet ones after that.

Level two, the boyfriend.
You’re twenty-two, and you don’t sit at the mall anymore. You’ve got four phones and forty girls you’re messaging at the same time.
You find them through their friends’ pictures, and you like everything they post. You tell her she’s prettier than all her friends, and way more mature than the boys she goes to school with.
This is intentional.
For weeks you’re the only one who gets her. You send her AirPods, and you tell her that her mom just doesn’t understand her.
Then you ask her to come out and see your car, and you get a thousand dollars for every girl who does.
One of them shows her mom the messages. By that night a detective is calling your number, so you snap the phone in half and throw it in a dumpster behind a Wendy’s.
Three months of work, gone, and you learn the second rule. If anybody’s watching her, you move on.

Level three, the driver.
You’re twenty-four, and they give you a black Suburban with the back windows tinted.
Most of the girls don’t come from the messages anymore, they come from parking lots. Sometimes a spotter ties a piece of string on her door handle, so you know which car is hers.
You park in the spot next to it with the engine running and the back door open a crack, at two in the afternoon at a Target, and broad daylight doesn’t bother you at all.
You never grab her. You walk up beside her like you know her, and you say her name, because you got it off her Instagram.
Most of them freeze. She opens her mouth and nothing comes out.
You get three thousand dollars a girl, and you pay cash for a Charger.
The older driver teaches you rule three. Daylight doesn’t matter, being seen does, and if anybody starts looking, you get back in the truck and leave her standing there.

Level four, the motel.
You’re twenty-seven, and you run a motel off the interstate. Twelve rooms, and the guy at the front desk is on the payroll.
The girls don’t leave. They don’t have their phones, they don’t know what town they’re in, and you’ve told every one of them what happens to her family if she runs.
Men find the rooms online. One girl brings in thousands of dollars a week, and you keep a cut of every one.
Your whole job is keeping it quiet. No fights, no noise, nobody calling the cops about the room next door.
A new driver gets greedy and takes a girl in front of a gas station full of people. By morning he’s gone, and nobody says his name again.
That’s the rule at this level. A girl is only worth that money if nobody saw you take her.

Level five, the boss.
You’re thirty-four. You’ve got six motels in three states and nineteen guys under you.
You wash the money through two car washes and a nail salon, and your wife thinks you sell cars.
You’ve got a house with a pool, and you sleep four hours a night.
You’ve thought of everything. The phones change every week. You’re untraceable.
You like the nice neighborhoods best, because those parents think it could never happen there.
And there’s one rule you give every new guy on his first day. If she makes a scene, you let her go, because there’s always a quieter one.

Level six, the bust.
It’s a Tuesday afternoon at a Target, and your newest driver walks up beside a girl at her car, just like you taught him.
She doesn’t freeze. She pulls something off her keys, and it’s louder than an ambulance siren, and it’s flashing.
Every head in that parking lot turns. A woman stops with her cart, two guys by the doors start walking over, and a man two rows over already has his phone up.
Your driver does exactly what you taught him. He lets her go, and he leaves.
But the man two rows over got the plate.
The plate gives them the driver, and the driver’s phone gives them the motel. The motel gives them the car washes, and the car washes give them you.
They come through your front door at five in the morning while your kids are asleep upstairs.
Eleven years, and it’s over in nine days.

Level seven, the cell.
You’re thirty-five, and you’re in a cell twenty-three hours a day, with the rest of your life to run it back.
You planned for everything. Her phone, her friends, the cops, the apps her mom watched her on.
You planned for pepper spray. You planned for tasers. You knew when to back off.
In eleven years, you never once planned for a girl who could make that much noise. Draw that much attention.
At your trial they hold it up in a plastic bag, this little alarm off a girl’s keychain. It’s called Palace Guard.
It’s a personal alarm made by a detective. It’s a hundred and thirty decibels, which is louder than an ambulance siren, with a strobe light, so nobody in that lot could pretend they didn’t see it, and all she had to do was pull one pin.
Her mom got it on a holiday sale, three of them and two more free, for less than twenty dollars each.
Your entire operation crumbled over a twenty dollar alarm.

Level eight, the new rule.
The business doesn’t end with you. Somebody’s running your motels before your trial is even over.
And somewhere tonight, a nineteen-year-old kid is sitting on a bench in a food court with a phone and a lemonade, learning the rules.
Never the one who looks back. If anybody’s watching her, move on. If she makes a scene, let her go.
And now there’s a new one, the one you learned too late. Never the girl with a Palace Guard on her keys.

That part is getting harder. Moms know this is the best way to stop people like you.

And right now the detective who makes them is doing a holiday sale, buy three and get two free, so it comes out to less than twenty dollars each. 

You’re glad you don’t have to worry about them anymore.
