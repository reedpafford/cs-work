Avatar: moms and grandmas of a non-verbal autistic child, about 3 to 12, who has already gotten out once (a new buyer for the brand, a test cohort).
Desire: sleeping through the night again, knowing she'll wake up the second he goes.
Angle: everything she bought tried to keep him in, and he got around all of it: locks he figured out, latches he reached with a chair, a chime she slept through, a tracker he chewed off that only showed where he already was. He can't call out and won't answer his name.
Mechanism: attention. You can't out-lock a kid who wants the water; the only thing that works is someone knowing the second he's out. The pin on a cord to his door: the door opens, the pin pulls, the house wakes while he's still on the porch. Off the door in the morning, onto her purse, the cord on his belt loop.
Rule: the siren is never on him (sound sensitivity, Reed, 2026-11-24): mounted high on the outside of the frame, quiet the second the pin goes back in. It's an alarm he sets off by leaving, never one he's expected to pull.

## Body
### Script
My sweet boy Owen, he’s non-verbal.
I knew this was coming, because he’s been eloping since the day he could walk.
My husband and I did all the prep we knew at the time. We changed the locks on the front door, and he figured those out in about a week.
We then put a latch at the very top of the door, where Owen couldn’t reach it. And we put up one of those little chimes that dings when the door opens.
We even got him a GPS tracker for his wrist, for forty dollars a month, and he chewed through the band the first day.
Some nights I slept on the floor outside his room, but that week I’d finally gone back to my own bed.
We thought we had covered every base. Then it happened.
I woke up a little after three, and the house was cold.
His bed was empty, and the front door was wide open.
He’d pushed a kitchen chair over to the door, climbed up, and undone the latch. I guess the chime went off, and I slept right through it.
I ran out barefoot screaming his name. He couldn't answer to his own name. I knew that. I did it anyway.
Then I started to panic. There’s a pond about three blocks behind our house. That had to be where he went.
I ran over there, trying not to imagine him… in the water.
I found him standing at the edge of the water in his pajamas, up to his ankles, looking at the reflection of the moon.
It gets deep about two steps out from where he was standing. Deep enough that had he wandered just a little bit further… I don’t even want to think about it.
I carried him home and sat on the kitchen floor holding him until the sun came up.
The next day a neighbor told me that I needed to keep a better eye on him. That made me so mad. I hadn’t slept a whole night in four years. She has no idea what it’s like.
A couple weeks later I was scrolling on Facebook, and I saw a video of a young man with autism explaining why kids like him elope, and he said something I can’t stop thinking about.
He said we’re not running away from you, we’re running toward something. The water, a light, a sound we like.
That’s when it hit me. Every lock I bought was trying to keep him in, and Owen was always going to find a way around a lock, because he had a reason.
What I needed was to know the second he got out. Something that wouldn’t fail to make me or anyone else aware of what was going on.
So I got a personal alarm, the kind girls put on their keys. You pull the pin, and it’s louder than an ambulance siren.
I hung it up high on the outside of his bedroom door frame and tied a little string from the pin to his door, so if that door opens at night, the pin comes out.
And I know what some of you are thinking, a siren, for him? Owen covers his ears when I run the vacuum.
That’s exactly why it’s up high and outside his room, never on him, and the second I push the pin back in, it’s quiet. It takes about three seconds.
Three weeks later, at two in the morning, it went off.
I was out of bed before I was even awake, and Owen was halfway down the hallway with his shoes in his hand.
He never made it to the front door. I pushed the pin back in, and I held him on the hallway floor until he calmed down, and I cried, because I’d actually been asleep.
It’s called Palace Guard. There’s no app, no monthly fee, and nothing he has to wear on his wrist.
I bought three and got two free, so it came out to less than twenty dollars each. One’s on his bedroom door, one’s on the back slider, one’s on my purse, and the other two went to my mother’s house, because that’s where we spend the holidays.
You can give it a try for 30 days, and if you don’t like it, or your child doesn’t respond well to it, you can send it back.
The stories I read about moms just like us losing our innocent babies break my heart. I got lucky once, and I wasn’t going to count on being lucky twice.
