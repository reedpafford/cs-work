## What a Movie is here
Same job as every ad: install the one belief (her daughter could be assaulted at any moment, and a personal alarm is how you stop it) and get the parent to buy. A movie does it with scenes the viewer watches instead of a story she's told. Length is never the problem; a boring scene is (principle 1). The winners hold 6 to 14 minutes because every scene either hurts, explains, or pays off.

## The skeleton (all four winners)
1. **Cold open = the wound, in the scene, in the first line of dialogue. Nobody talks to the camera (Reed, RP5, 2026-11-09: "It is a movie, they don't talk to the viewers.").** Granny pats her stomach: "When are you due?" The guys in the diner: "That's Bear's wife." The hook is said by one character to another, and the viewer understands it on one listen. No narrator line, no V.O. over the hook, not even one sentence. The keyword is spoken by whoever would really say it: the officer on the phone, the witness calling 911, the detective at the table (hooks.md still rules: predator, abducted, assaulted, never a stand-in). The plate carries the rest. For us it's a flash-forward to a moment in the body.
2. **The wound happens in public.** Someone else does it, out loud, with witnesses: the whole bleacher section boos; the mean friend says it in her ear; the club knew for months. For us: the manager ten feet away, the lot full of people, the store that knew for two weeks.
3. **The private low point, mostly silent.** Alone at the truck, the bathroom mirror, the car in the driveway with the engine off. One or two V.O. lines over held shots of a face. This is where the lead says the one line that names the identity the wound broke ("I was the pretty one my whole life." · "I used to be the scariest man in that club.").
4. **The expert, in a long dialogue scene.** A friend who used to have it, a guy at the gym, a boxing coach, a 92-year-old father. Always someone who lived it, never a salesman. This is the longest scene in every winner (two to three minutes) and the only place long speeches are allowed. Ours: the detective at the kitchen table.
5. **Objections are spoken by the lead and answered in the scene.** "I cut the wine, I did keto." · "My doctor says it's normal." · "Drops? You're messing with me." · "I know the physiology." The expert knocks each one down. The viewer's own doubts come out of the lead's mouth, so they get answered before she has them. Ours: "She broke her brother's nose. Why couldn't she just scream?"
6. **The expert takes the blame off.** "You're not weak, brother. You're backed up." · "You don't got a weight problem. You got sludge." Ours: "Freezing isn't weakness. It happens to almost every girl, the tough ones too."
7. **The mechanism gets a cutaway or a screen.** The winners cut to a 3D animation of the clogged drain. Ours is the parking lot footage on the detective's laptop: no sound, every head turns.
8. **The product changes hands on camera.** Dani walks to her car and comes back with the bottle and closes Ashley's fingers over it. The bottle alone on the locker room bench. The dose is said out loud in the scene. Ours: the alarm clipped on her keys (planted early), the pin, the strobe.
9. **The rival gets named by the expert, never by the narrator.** "Don't grab whatever pops up online, most of them are weak doses and fillers." · "Most companies sell pale yellow garlic oil and call it aged garlic." Ours: "the cheap colorful ones in evidence bags."
10. **A dip before the climb.** The person who should believe doesn't: the husband on his phone ("Ash, you've tried everything"), the wife ("You've said that before"). The lead answers with a deadline ("Give me six weeks").
11. **Proof over time, through other people, mostly silent, with title cards.** "5 WEEKS OUT" · "WEEK 2" · "Two months later." A coworker, the tailor ("we need to take it in"), a club brother ("you dropping weight, Bear?"). The lead never says it works; other people notice.
12. **The payoff happens in the same kind of place as the wound, with the same people.** The next wedding, the same mean friend. The same bleacher guys at the gas station. Cody at the rally. The patient who refused the pills comes back. The line that wounded comes back and lands the other way ("Careful, Barbie, somebody's going to ask when you're due." "Let him ask.").
13. **The close is the lead's V.O. over product shots:** the story in one sentence aimed at the viewer ("If you were always the pretty one and somewhere along the way you got puffy…"), the mechanism in one sentence, the dose, the offer, the guarantee, "if the button's still there, grab it." Ours adds the "Maybe" lines (song.md), the regret beat, the share CTA, and "Don't wait."

## Perspective
**It's a movie: the story is told by people talking to each other, never to the viewer, until the close.** The lead is on screen and is the one the movie is about. Mid-story V.O. is only a short thought in the moment, in the lead's head ("Weird. But whatever." · "Something was moving. I didn't tell anybody."), never narration that explains or sets up ("you need to know who I am first" is out). Anything a narrator would explain becomes a scene or a line between characters: her toughness is her brother on the floor yelling "she broke my nose"; the freeze is her answer when the officer asks "Did you scream?"; the mother's guilt is said to the daughter. **The close is the only place the lead speaks to the viewer**, in V.O. over product shots, the way all four winners end.
On RP5 Reed called for the daughter's side, so Paige is the lead and the close is hers; the mother's regret and the product facts come out in scenes (Dana at the detective's table). Untested whether the daughter's or the mother's voice sells harder in the close.

- `[SCENE n — place, time]` opens each scene. `(…)` is what the camera sees, one visual per line (principle 2), so a silent beat is its own line: `(she looks at the manager)` · `(she opens her mouth, then closes it)`. `Name: line` is speech. `(V.O.)` is voice-over, `(O.S.)` off screen, `(on the phone)` for a voice on a call.
- **Silence is written, not implied.** The winners run 15 to 30 second stretches with no words: the montage, the low point, the payoff walk-in. Write each shot as its own direction line so the editor knows the beat is there.
- **V2 and V3 change only the cold open:** a different flash-forward from the body, then `(then V1 from SCENE 2 to the end)`.

## Writing it
1. **Short lines, people talking past each other.** Three to ten words is normal in a scene; interruptions and half-answers are how people talk. Only the expert gets runs of several sentences. Nobody tells someone what both already know.
2. **Every speaker gets a register, and the persona registers still hold.** The daughter rationalizes before she worries ("Weird. But whatever."); the mother is cautious, not dumb, never controlling; the detective is flat and quiet on the darkest lines and says "look." A new character gets three words they'd say and one they never would, decided before their first line.
3. **Plant the product early, pay it off late.** The winners hand it over mid-story; ours is already on her keys, so show it going on (the August driveway, "what if it goes off in class?") before the danger, and call that line back after ("Because it doesn't go off in class.").
4. **Dramatic irony, cut in.** The viewer sees where the parent was while it happened: the dot on the phone, the oven preheating. An intercut scene, not a sentence after.
5. **The mechanism is on screen and matches the hardware:** a keychain, a pin, 130 dB, the strobe. A viewer believes what she sees over what she's told.
6. **Roller coaster across scenes.** A two-minute kitchen table, then four three-second shots. Three scenes of the same length or mood in a row is a rewrite (principle 7).
7. **Profanity:** the winners curse constantly ("Normal, my ass," "didn't do shit"). Ours doesn't until Reed says so; the buyer is a woman 45 to 70.
8. **The word rule covers everything:** speech, directions and plates. Never the banned words; "assaulted" and "abducted" are the ceiling; death is implied ("I can't talk about her," "only two came home") (principle 8).
9. **The image rule is now in the script.** Directions are the visuals, so nothing in a `(…)` line shows or suggests a sexual threat: no girl pinned, no hand over a mouth in close-up, no drink being spiked, no weapon in frame. Men walking toward her, the open van door, her face: the threat lives in distance and in what she can't do.

## Before handing it back
